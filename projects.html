Projects

Possible course projects, grouped by lecture. Each one lists a type (theory, experimental or expository), a concrete first step and a few key references. They are starting points: you can narrow, combine or redirect them. Talk to me before you commit to one.

A good project produces one of the following: a clean proof of a result (new or not, but written in our language), a careful experiment that tests a theoretical prediction, or a self-contained exposition of a paper that goes beyond the lectures.

Lecture 8: Spectral clustering and eigenvector perturbation

Notation follows the Lecture 8 slides and the Davis–Kahan note: \(P=D^{-1}A\), \(\ \ell^2(D)\), planted clusters \(S_1,\dots,S_k\), ideal walk \(P_0\) (cut edges turned into self-loops), \(\ \delta_0\) the ideal gap, \(\ \Phi(S_j)\) the conductance of cluster \(j\).

1. Davis–Kahan as stability of a minimizer

Theory / expository.

The lecture proves Davis–Kahan with a Sylvester equation. There is also a variational route: the top-\(k\) eigenspace of \(A\) is the minimizer of \(f_A(\Pi)=-\operatorname{tr}(\Pi A)\) over rank-\(k\) projections, so a perturbation of \(A\) is a perturbation of an optimization problem, and perturbation bounds follow from how fast \(f_A\) grows away from its minimizer. This route appears in the statistics literature, through the “curvature lemma” of Vu–Cho–Lei–Rohe (2013), and is closely related to Yu–Wang–Samworth’s variant of Davis–Kahan.

Questions.

  1. Present the curvature lemma and a variational proof of a Frobenius-norm Davis–Kahan bound in the notation of the course. Compare the constants with Corollary 3.3 of the lecture.
  2. Literature. Find out precisely which parts of this argument are in Vu et al. and in Yu–Wang–Samworth, and in what form.
  3. Geometry. \(f_A\) is geodesically convex on the Grassmannian only near its minimizer (Alimisis–Vandereycken). What does a perturbation argument actually need from \(f_A\): convexity, strong convexity along geodesics, or something weaker?
  4. Operator norm. Can a variational argument give the operator-norm bound \(\|\sin\Theta\|\lesssim\|E\|/\delta_0\)?
  5. Beyond the top-\(k\) block. Extend the argument to an interior block of the spectrum, and to \(\ell^2(D)\).

First step. Read the curvature lemma in Vu et al. and the proof of the main theorem in Yu–Wang–Samworth.

References. Vu, Cho, Lei & Rohe, Fantope projection and selection (NeurIPS 2013). · Yu, Wang & Samworth, A useful variant of the Davis–Kahan theorem for statisticians (Biometrika 2015). · Alimisis & Vandereycken, Geodesic convexity of the symmetric eigenvalue problem and convergence of steepest descent (JOTA 2024; arXiv:2209.03480).

2. Bounds that survive hubs

Theory + experimental.

The lecture has two bounds for planted clusters. Davis–Kahan is linear in the perturbation but pays for the worst vertex (\(\varepsilon_{\rm out}\)). The energy bound \(\|\sin\Theta\|_F^2\le\sum_j\Phi(S_j)/\mu_{k+1}(G)\) uses only average quantities but has a square-root rate and needs the gap of the observed graph.

Questions.

  1. Is there a hub-robust bound, depending only on average quantities such as \(\sum_j\Phi(S_j)\), that uses the gap \(\delta_0\) of the ideal graph instead?
  2. Is there a bound that is both linear in the perturbation and depends only on average quantities, e.g. on \(\sum_j\Phi(S_j)\) instead of \(\max_u d_{\rm out}(u)/d(u)\)? Or construct a family of graphs showing that no such bound can hold.
  3. Regularization for deterministic graphs. In random graphs, trimming or down-weighting high-degree vertices restores concentration (Le–Levina–Vershynin). Does a deterministic analogue (e.g., adding \(\tau/n\) to every entry of \(A\), or capping \(d_{\rm out}(u)\)) make Davis–Kahan hub-robust?

First step. Use the planted-clusters tab of the Lecture 8 demo to compare the two bounds on spread and hub placements, and look for graphs where both are loose.

References. Peng, Sun & Zanetti, Partitioning well-clustered graphs: spectral clustering works! (COLT 2015; SIAM J. Comput. 2017). · Le, Levina & Vershynin, Concentration and regularization of random graphs (Random Structures Algorithms 2017).

3. How sharp is Davis–Kahan in the SBM?

Theory + experimental.

In the SBM experiments of the lecture, the actual \(\|\sin\Theta\|\) matches the spiked-random-matrix prediction \(\sigma/\delta_0\) almost exactly, while Davis–Kahan gives about \(4\sigma/\delta_0\).

Questions.

  1. Derive the prediction for the two-block SBM from the theory of finite-rank perturbations of random matrices (BBP transition, Benaych-Georges–Nadakuditi): the location of the outlier eigenvalue and the limiting \(\|\sin\Theta\|\), including the block structure of the noise variance.
  2. Account for the factor \(4\): which factor of \(2\) comes from Weyl, and which from \(\|E\|\approx2\sigma\)? Is there a deterministic perturbation bound that captures the “random direction” of \(E\), e.g. by using \(\|EV\|\) in place of \(\|E\|\)?
  3. Test the predictions numerically for \(k>2\) and unequal block sizes.

First step. Reproduce the sweep in the demo at \(n=2000\) in Python, and overlay the BBP formula.

References. Baik, Ben Arous & Péché, Phase transition of the largest eigenvalue for nonnull complex sample covariance matrices (Ann. Probab. 2005). · Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi, The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of finite, low rank perturbations of large random matrices (Adv. Math. 2011).

4. Sparse graphs: why spectral clustering fails, and how to fix it

Experimental.

At constant average degree (\(p=a/n\), \(q=b/n\)), high-degree vertices create eigenvalues that swamp the community signal, and plain spectral clustering fails well above the information-theoretic threshold \((a-b)^2>2(a+b)\).

Questions.

  1. Show the failure empirically: plot the spectrum of \(A\) and the localized top eigenvectors.
  2. Implement and compare three fixes: regularized spectral clustering (\(A+\tau\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/n\)), the non-backtracking matrix, and the Bethe Hessian. Measure the overlap with the planted partition as \((a-b)^2/(2(a+b))\) crosses \(1\).
  3. Explain, in the language of this course, why each fix works.

First step. Generate SBMs with \(n=10^4\), \(a+b=6\), and plot the top eigenvectors of \(A\) and of the regularized Laplacian.

References. Krzakala et al., Spectral redemption in clustering sparse networks (PNAS 2013). · Saade, Krzakala & Zdeborová, Spectral clustering of graphs with the Bethe Hessian (NeurIPS 2014). · Amini, Chen, Bickel & Levina, Pseudo-likelihood methods for community detection in large sparse networks (Ann. Statist. 2013). · Le, Levina & Vershynin (2017).

5. From counting bad vertices to exact recovery

Theory / expository.

Davis–Kahan plus Markov bounds the number of misclustered vertices. Exact recovery needs every row of the embedding to be close to its center, i.e., a bound on \(\|\hat VO-V\|_{2\to\infty}\).

Questions.

  1. Present the leave-one-out argument of Abbe–Fan–Wang–Zhong for the two-block SBM, and show that spectral clustering recovers the partition exactly when \(p=\frac{a\ln n}n\), \(q=\frac{b\ln n}n\) and \((\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2>2\).
  2. Compare with the deterministic \(2\to\infty\) bounds of Cape–Tang–Priebe. Which assumptions does each approach need?

First step. Read the introduction of Abbe–Fan–Wang–Zhong and write out their main entrywise bound in the special case of the two-block SBM.

References. Abbe, Fan, Wang & Zhong, Entrywise eigenvector analysis of random matrices with low expected rank (Ann. Statist. 2020). · Cape, Tang & Priebe, The two-to-infinity norm and singular subspace geometry with applications to high-dimensional statistics (Ann. Statist. 2019). · Abbe, Community detection and stochastic block models: recent developments (JMLR 2018).

6. Rounding: what does \(k\)-means actually guarantee?

Theory.

The lecture counts bad vertices with respect to the true centers. An algorithm only has the \(k\)-means solution, and \(k\)-means is NP-hard to optimize exactly.

Questions.

  1. Prove that a \((1+\epsilon)\)-approximate weighted \(k\)-means solution in \(\ell^2(D)\) misclusters a volume fraction \(O\big((1+\epsilon)\,\|\hat FO-F\|_{D,F}^2\big)\), following Lei–Rinaldo, Lemma 5.3.
  2. Compare with the “proximity condition” analysis of Kumar–Kannan and Awasthi–Sheffet, which works directly with the spectral norm of the perturbation.
  3. Degree heterogeneity. In the degree-corrected SBM, compare NJW row normalization, the random-walk embedding with weighted \(k\)-means, and SCORE (ratios of eigenvectors), theoretically or experimentally.

First step. Prove part 1 for exact \(k\)-means (\(\epsilon=0\)) and \(k=2\).

References. Lei & Rinaldo, Consistency of spectral clustering in stochastic block models (Ann. Statist. 2015). · Kumar & Kannan, Clustering with spectral norm and the k-means algorithm (FOCS 2010). · Awasthi & Sheffet, Improved spectral-norm bounds for clustering (APPROX 2012). · Qin & Rohe, Regularized spectral clustering under the degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel (NeurIPS 2013). · Jin, Fast community detection by SCORE (Ann. Statist. 2015).

7. Directed graphs and non-reversible chains

Theory / expository.

The proof of Davis–Kahan uses self-adjointness in one place: \(\hat V_\perp^\top\hat A=\hat\Lambda_2\hat V_\perp^\top\). For a non-reversible walk the eigenvectors are not orthogonal in any fixed inner product, and the inverse of the Sylvester operator can be large even when the gap is large.

Questions.

  1. Construct a family of non-normal matrices with a fixed eigengap whose invariant subspaces are arbitrarily unstable. Relate the instability to pseudospectra.
  2. Present a spectral clustering method for directed graphs, e.g. Hermitian adjacency matrices (Cucuringu–Li–Sun–Zanetti), and explain which perturbation theory it relies on.
  3. Is there a variational (Project 1) version for non-reversible chains, e.g. via the symmetric part of the generator?

First step. Compute the invariant subspaces of a perturbed Jordan-type block numerically and compare their movement with \(\|E\|/\text{gap}\).

References. Trefethen & Embree, Spectra and Pseudospectra (Princeton 2005). · Cucuringu, Li, Sun & Zanetti, Hermitian matrices for clustering directed graphs: insights and applications (AISTATS 2020). · The course note on non-reversible chains.

8. Matrix concentration from scratch

Expository.

Questions.

  1. Prove matrix Bernstein in full, including the step that uses Lieb’s concavity theorem. You may take Lieb’s theorem as given, but explain what it says.
  2. Show where the dimension factor and the \(\sqrt{\log n}\) loss come from, and why the Ahlswede–Winter (Golden–Thompson) argument loses much more for the SBM.
  3. Present the Feige–Ofek argument that removes the \(\log n\) factor when \(d\gtrsim\log n\), or the light/heavy-pairs method behind it.

References. Tropp, An introduction to matrix concentration inequalities (Found. Trends Mach. Learn. 2015). · Ahlswede & Winter, Strong converse for identification via quantum channels (IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 2002). · Feige & Ofek, Spectral techniques applied to sparse random graphs (Random Structures Algorithms 2005).

9. Oja’s method for streaming PCA

Theory + experimental.

Oja’s method, \(W_{t+1}=\operatorname{qr}(W_t+\eta_tx_tx_t^\top W_t)\), estimates the top-\(k\) eigenspace of a covariance \(\Sigma\) from a stream of samples. It can be read as stochastic Riemannian gradient ascent on \(Q\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(Q\Sigma)\) over the Grassmannian. The classical benchmark is the offline estimator, whose asymptotic error for Gaussian data is given by Anderson’s formula \[ t\cdot\mathbb E\|\sin\Theta\|_F^2\ \to\ \sum_{i\le k<j}\frac{\lambda_i\lambda_j}{(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^2}. \]

Questions.

  1. Show that one step of Oja is, to first order in \(\eta_t\), a retracted stochastic Riemannian gradient step. Compute the Hessian and the gradient-noise covariance at the optimum in tangent coordinates, and recover Anderson’s formula from them.
  2. Present the analysis of Jain et al. (one direction) or Allen-Zhu–Li (general \(k\)). How do they handle the start, when the iterate is far from the optimum and near the saddles?
  3. Which properties of the objective (geodesic convexity, Polyak–Łojasiewicz, quadratic growth) hold on the Grassmannian, and where? Which ones does a convergence proof for Oja need?
  4. Experiment. Compare plain Oja (step \(a/t\)), Oja with Polyak–Ruppert averaging and the offline estimator against Anderson’s constant, on spectra with unequal gaps \(\lambda_i-\lambda_j\).

First step. Simulate the offline estimator and Oja with \(n=10\), \(k=2\) and a Gaussian spectrum, and plot \(t\cdot\mathbb E\|\sin\Theta\|_F^2\) against Anderson’s limit.

References. Jain, Jin, Kakade, Netrapalli & Sidford, Streaming PCA: matching matrix Bernstein and near-optimal finite sample guarantees for Oja’s algorithm (COLT 2016). · Allen-Zhu & Li, First efficient convergence for streaming k-PCA: a global, gap-free, and near-optimal rate (FOCS 2017). · Hardt & Price, The noisy power method: a meta algorithm with applications (NeurIPS 2014). · Tripuraneni, Flammarion, Bach & Jordan, Averaging stochastic gradient descent on Riemannian manifolds (COLT 2018). · Karimi, Nutini & Schmidt, Linear convergence of gradient and proximal-gradient methods under the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition (ECML-PKDD 2016). · Anderson, Asymptotic theory for principal component analysis (Ann. Math. Statist. 1963).

10. Online PCA

Theory + experimental. Bridges Lecture 8 and Lecture 10 (matrix multiplicative weights).

In online PCA an adversary reveals PSD matrices \(X_t\) with \(\|X_t\|\le1\), the learner plays a rank-\(k\) projection \(Q_t\) before seeing \(X_t\) and gains \(\operatorname{tr}(Q_tX_t)\), and regret is measured against the best fixed projection. Matrix multiplicative weights (matrix exponentiated gradient) achieves regret of order \(\sqrt T\), logarithmic in the dimension (Warmuth–Kuzmin). Follow the leader (FTL), which plays the top-\(k\) projection of \(S_{t-1}=\sum_{s<t}X_s\), can fail badly against an adversary.

Questions.

  1. Construct an adversarial sequence on which FTL has linear regret.
  2. What regret does FTL get on i.i.d. data whose covariance has an eigengap? Relate the answer to the curved-constraint-set results of Huang–Lattimore–György–Szepesvári.
  3. Best of both worlds. Can an algorithm get the better of the two behaviors without knowing whether the data has a gap?
  4. Experiment. Compare FTL, matrix multiplicative weights and Oja’s method (online gradient ascent on the Grassmannian) on stochastic data with and without a gap, and on adversarial sequences.

First step. Implement FTL and matrix multiplicative weights for \(n=10\), \(k=2\) on Gaussian data with a population gap, and plot the regret of both against \(T\).

References. Warmuth & Kuzmin, Randomized online PCA algorithms with regret bounds that are logarithmic in the dimension (JMLR 2008). · Nie, Kotłowski & Warmuth, Online PCA with optimal regret (JMLR 2016). · Huang, Lattimore, György & Szepesvári, Following the leader and fast rates in online linear prediction: curved constraint sets and other regularities (JMLR 2017). · Allen-Zhu & Li, Follow the compressed leader: faster online learning of eigenvectors and faster MMWU (ICML 2017).

Lecture 9: Electrical flows and fast Laplacian solvers

Notation follows the Lecture 9 slides: resistances \(r_e=1/w_e\), demand \(\chi\), energy \(\mathcal E(f)=\sum_er_ef_e^2\), spanning tree \(T\), stretch \(\mathrm{st}_T(e)\), cycle resistance \(R_e\), \(\tau(T)=\sum_{e\notin T}R_e/r_e\). Code for the solvers, the centroid data structure and approximate elimination is in lectures/lecture09/scripts/core.py and in the demo.

1. Accelerated cycle toggling

Theory + experimental.

Lee–Sidford accelerate randomized coordinate descent so that the iteration count depends on \(\sum_i\sqrt{L_i/\mu}\) instead of \(\sum_iL_i/\mu\), and apply it to KOSZ: \(\sqrt{m\,\tau(T)}\) toggles instead of \(\tau(T)\).

Questions.

  1. Present accelerated RCD for a quadratic with a complete proof (estimate sequences or a potential-function argument), in the notation of the lecture.
  2. An accelerated step updates a linear combination of two or three sequences. Show how to implement it with the centroid structure of the lecture in \(O(\log n)\) time per step (Lee–Sidford maintain the iterates in a changing basis).
  3. Experiment. Implement it on grids and random graphs and compare with plain KOSZ and CG. Does the \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) improvement appear at practical sizes?

First step. Read Lee–Sidford §§3–4 and write accelerated RCD for a small dense quadratic; check the rate numerically.

References. Lee & Sidford, Efficient accelerated coordinate descent methods and faster algorithms for solving linear systems (FOCS 2013). · Nesterov, Efficiency of coordinate descent methods on huge-scale optimization problems (SIAM J. Optim. 2012). · Allen-Zhu, Qu, Richtárik & Yuan, Even faster accelerated coordinate descent using non-uniform sampling (ICML 2016).

2. Cycle toggling in practice

Experimental.

In the lecture’s experiments KOSZ loses to CG by a large factor on unit-weight grids and wins on weighted grids, while PCG with the same tree wins everywhere.

Questions.

  1. Which part of the KOSZ bound is loose on these examples: \(\lambda_{\min}(M)\ge1\), \(\lambda_{\max}(M)\le\operatorname{tr}M\), or the start bound? Measure the spectrum of \(M\).
  2. Try variants: sampling without replacement (epochs over a random permutation), sampling \(\propto\) current \(\Delta_e^2/r_e\) (greedy / Gauss–Southwell), block toggles of several cycles at once, and using KOSZ as a preconditioner for CG.
  3. Test on real networks (road networks, meshes, social graphs) with trees from a practical low-stretch heuristic.

First step. Reproduce the convergence figure of the lecture with scripts/make_figures.py, then add the epoch-based variant.

References. Kelner, Orecchia, Sidford & Zhu (STOC 2013). · Boman, Deweese & Gilbert, An empirical comparison of graph Laplacian solvers (ALENEX 2016). · Spielman’s Laplacians.jl for baselines.

3. Low-stretch spanning trees

Theory / expository + experimental.

Questions.

  1. Present the AKPW construction and its \(\exp(O(\sqrt{\log n\log\log n}))\) bound, then the star decomposition of Elkin–Emek–Spielman–Teng, and explain what Abraham–Neiman’s petal decomposition improves.
  2. Prove the \(\Omega(\log n)\) lower bound on the average stretch of any spanning tree of the grid (Alon–Karp–Peleg–West).
  3. Experiment. Implement one construction and compare its total stretch with BFS, maximum-weight and random (Wilson) spanning trees. How does the stretch of a uniformly random spanning tree compare? (Its expected stretch is related to effective resistances: \(\Pr[e\in T]=w_eR_{\rm eff}(e)\).)

First step. Compute the stretch of uniformly random spanning trees on grids of increasing size and compare with the recursive tree of the lecture.

References. Alon, Karp, Peleg & West (SIAM J. Comput. 1995). · Elkin, Emek, Spielman & Teng, Lower-stretch spanning trees (STOC 2005). · Abraham & Neiman, Using petal-decompositions to build a low stretch spanning tree (STOC 2012). · Wilson, Generating random spanning trees more quickly than the cover time (STOC 1996).

4. Approximate Gaussian elimination

Theory + experimental.

Questions.

  1. Prove a version of the Kyng–Sachdeva theorem: with \(\rho=O(\log^2n)\) splits and a random elimination order, \(\tfrac12L\preceq\tilde L\preceq\tfrac32L\) w.h.p. You may take matrix Freedman as given; state it precisely.
  2. Why is the order important? Give an order for which clique sampling produces a disconnected approximation with constant probability (hint: a star eliminated center-first).
  3. Experiment. Compare random order, minimum-degree order and nested dissection on grids and random graphs, for the approximation quality and the number of nonzeros. Compare with the robust variant of Gao–Kyng–Spielman.

First step. Run approx_cholesky in scripts/core.py with different orders and plot the spectrum of \(L^{+/2}\tilde LL^{+/2}\).

References. Kyng & Sachdeva, Approximate Gaussian elimination for Laplacians: fast, sparse, and simple (FOCS 2016). · Kyng, Approximate Gaussian elimination (PhD thesis, Yale 2017). · Gao, Kyng & Spielman, Robust and practical solution of Laplacian equations by approximate elimination (arXiv:2303.00709). · Tropp, Freedman’s inequality for matrix martingales (Electron. Commun. Probab. 2011).

5. Primal vs. dual coordinate descent

Theory.

KOSZ runs coordinate descent on flows (the cycle space). One can instead run it on potentials, one vertex at a time (Gauss–Seidel / randomized Kaczmarz on the dual).

Questions.

  1. Apply the RCD theorem of the lecture to \(\max_x\mathcal D(x)\) with vertex coordinates. What is the rate, and why does it depend on \(\lambda_2\)?
  2. Explain the asymmetry: why does a spanning tree give a good basis of the cycle space, but no analogous basis of the potentials?
  3. Read the “dual randomized Kaczmarz” view of KOSZ: KOSZ can be read as Kaczmarz on the KVL constraints. Make the correspondence precise.

References. Strohmer & Vershynin (J. Fourier Anal. Appl. 2009). · Leventhal & Lewis (Math. Oper. Res. 2010). · Gower & Richtárik, Randomized iterative methods for linear systems (SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. 2015).

6. Electrical flows as an algorithmic primitive

Expository.

Questions.

  1. Present the Christiano–Kelner–Mądry–Spielman–Teng algorithm for approximate maximum flow: multiplicative weights over electrical flows, and the role of the solver.
  2. Present Spielman–Srivastava’s estimation of all effective resistances with \(O(\log n/\varepsilon^2)\) solves via Johnson–Lindenstrauss.
  3. Optional: survey how electrical flows enter interior-point methods for flows (Mądry 2013) and the later almost-linear-time max-flow algorithm (Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg & Sachdeva 2022), which replaces them by other primitives.

References. Christiano, Kelner, Mądry, Spielman & Teng, Electrical flows, Laplacian systems, and faster approximation of maximum flow in undirected graphs (STOC 2011). · Spielman & Srivastava, Graph sparsification by effective resistances (STOC 2008). · Mądry, Navigating central path with electrical flows (FOCS 2013). · Vishnoi, Lx = b (FnT TCS 2013).
