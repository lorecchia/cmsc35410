Laplacian systems

The problem and how we measure error

\(G=(V,E,w)\) connected, \(n\) vertices, \(m\) edges; resistances \(r_e=1/w_e\). Orient each edge \(e=(u,v)\) and set \(b_e=\mathbf 1_u-\mathbf 1_v\) (the sign is immaterial; Lecture 2’s \(\nabla\) is \(-B\)). \[B=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\ b_e^\top\\ \vdots\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{m\times n},\qquad L=B^\top WB=\sum_e w_e\,b_eb_e^\top,\qquad W=\operatorname{diag}(w),\ R=W^{-1}.\]

  • \(\ker L=\operatorname{span}(\mathbf 1)\), so \(Lx=b\) is solvable iff \(b\perp\mathbf 1\), and \(x^*=L^+b\) is the solution orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\).
  • Error in the \(L\)-norm: \(\ \|x\|_L^2=x^\top Lx=\sum_ew_e(x_u-x_v)^2\). Adding a constant vector to \(x\) does not change it.

\(x\) is an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate solution if \(\ \|x-x^*\|_L\le\varepsilon\,\|x^*\|_L\).

  • Reductions. Symmetric diagonally dominant (SDD) systems reduce to Laplacian systems on a graph of twice the size (Gremban 1996). Everything today applies to SDD matrices.

~3 min. A solution in the L-norm can be converted to ℓ2 error at the cost of an additive ½ log κ in log(1/ε), which is why nobody cares about the choice inside nearly-linear-time statements.

Solving \(Lx=b\) is a minimization problem

\[\min_{x\in\mathbb R^n}\ f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Lx-b^\top x=\tfrac12\sum_ew_e\,(b_e^\top x)^2-b^\top x .\]

  • First-order optimality. \(f\) is convex (\(L\succeq0\)) and \(\nabla f(x)=Lx-b\). So \(x\) is a minimizer iff \(Lx=b\).
    • If \(b\not\perp\mathbf 1\), then \(f\) is unbounded below along \(\mathbf 1\): no solution.
  • Why the \(L\)-norm. \(f\) is quadratic and \(\nabla f(x^*)=0\), so the Taylor expansion at \(x^*\) is exact: \[f(x)-f(x^*)=\tfrac12(x-x^*)^\top L(x-x^*)=\tfrac12\|x-x^*\|_L^2 .\] The \(L\)-norm is the norm in which \(f\) measures progress.

~2 min. Setup for everything else today: the solver can be seen as unconstrained optimization over potentials (gradient descent, Krylov, preconditioning) or as constrained optimization over flows.

The dual of Laplacian solving is the flow problem

Lagrangian duality, through \(B\): give the edge differences their own variable \(y=Bx\in\mathbb R^E\): \[\min_{x,\,y}\ \tfrac12y^\top Wy-b^\top x\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad y=Bx .\] Attach a multiplier \(\phi\in\mathbb R^E\) to the constraint: \[\mathcal L(x,y,\phi)=\tfrac12y^\top Wy-b^\top x+\phi^\top(Bx-y)=\Big[\tfrac12y^\top Wy-\phi^\top y\Big]+\big(B^\top\phi-b\big)^\top x .\]

  • Minimize over \(y\): the bracket is minimized at \(y=W^{-1}\phi=R\phi\) (Ohm’s law), with value \(-\tfrac12\phi^\top R\phi=-\mathcal E(\phi)\), where \(\mathcal E(\phi)=\tfrac12\sum_er_e\phi_e^2\) is the energy.
  • Minimize over \(x\): the term is linear in \(x\), so the minimum is \(-\infty\) unless \(B^\top\phi=b\), and \(0\) if \(B^\top\phi=b\).
  • Dual function \(q(\phi)=\min_{x,y}\mathcal L\), so the dual problem is \[\max_\phi\ q(\phi)\ =\ \max_{B^\top\phi=b}\ -\mathcal E(\phi)\ =\ -\min_{B^\top\phi=b}\ \mathcal E(\phi) .\]
  • The dual is the flow problem: route the demand \(b\) with minimum energy. Strong duality holds (convex quadratic objective, linear constraints, feasible), so \(\min_xf(x)=-\min_{B^\top\phi=b}\mathcal E(\phi)\).
variable constraint objective
primal potentials \(x\in\mathbb R^V\) none \(\tfrac12\|x\|_L^2-b^\top x\)
dual flows \(\phi\in\mathbb R^E\) \(B^\top\phi=b\) \(\mathcal E(\phi)\)

~2 min. Optional. Poisson, not Cauchy: the Laplacian solve is the elliptic (steady-state) problem, while heat flow is its time-dependent counterpart. The graph case with no boundary is the pure Neumann problem, which is why the solution is only defined up to a constant.

The continuous picture

Poisson on the unit square with insulated sides (Neumann), one source and one sink, \(\sigma\equiv1\).

  • Colour, level sets: the potential \(u\), solving \(-\Delta u=\rho\). It is high at the source and low at the sink.
  • Streamlines: the flow \(\phi=-\sigma\nabla u\), which is orthogonal to the level sets. It leaves the source, enters the sink, and satisfies \(\operatorname{div}\phi=\rho\).
  • Dirichlet principle: \(u\) minimizes \(\tfrac12\int|\nabla u|^2-\int\rho u\).
  • Thomson principle: among all fields with \(\operatorname{div}\phi=\rho\), this \(\phi\) has least energy \(\tfrac12\int|\phi|^2/\sigma\).

Electrical flows

A flow \(f\in\mathbb R^E\) routes the demand \(\chi\) (with \(\mathbf 1^\top\chi=0\)) if \(B^\top f=\chi\): net outflow \(\chi_u\) at every \(u\). Energy: \(\ \mathcal E(f)=\tfrac12\sum_er_ef_e^2\).

Thomson’s principle. \(\displaystyle\min_{B^\top f=\chi}\mathcal E(f)=\tfrac12\chi^\top L^+\chi\), attained by the electrical flow \(f^*=WBx^*\), \(\ x^*=L^+\chi\) (Ohm’s law: \(f_e=w_e(x_u-x_v)\)).

Weak duality. For any \(f\) routing \(\chi\) and any potentials \(x\), let \(\ \mathcal D(x)=\chi^\top x-\tfrac12x^\top Lx\). Using \(\chi^\top x=f^\top Bx=\sum_ef_e\,b_e^\top x\), \[\boxed{\ \mathcal E(f)-\mathcal D(x)=\frac12\sum_e\frac{\big(r_ef_e-b_e^\top x\big)^2}{r_e}\ \ge0\ }\] Equality iff \(r_ef_e=b_e^\top x\) on every edge (Ohm). The flow \(WBx^*\) routes \(B^\top WBx^*=Lx^*=\chi\) and has equality. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dual error = solver error: \(\mathcal D(x)=-f(x)\) for the objective \(f\) of the previous slide (with \(b=\chi\)), so \(\ \mathcal D(x^*)-\mathcal D(x)=\tfrac12\|x-x^*\|_L^2\). We will use the boxed duality gap twice.

~4 min. Physics: Kirchhoff’s current law is B^T f = χ; Kirchhoff’s voltage law says the potential drops r_e f_e sum to zero around every cycle (equivalently, they are b_e^T x for some x). Thomson: of all flows obeying KCL, the one also obeying KVL minimizes energy. The gap identity says: the gap is exactly how badly Ohm’s law fails, edge by edge, measured in the right norm. Check: 2(D(x) − D(x)) = xᵀLx* − 2x*ᵀLx + xᵀLx. Numerically checked in scripts/verify_bounds.py §1 and §6.

Effective resistance

\[R_{\rm eff}(s,t)=(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t)^\top L^+(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t)=\text{twice the energy }\mathcal E\text{ of the unit }s\text{–}t\text{ electrical flow}=x^*_s-x^*_t,\quad x^*=L^+(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t).\]

  • A metric, and in fact an \(\ell_2^2\) metric: \(\ R_{\rm eff}(s,t)=\big\|L^{+/2}(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t)\big\|^2\) (compare the cut-cone and ARV in Lecture 6).
  • Rayleigh monotonicity: increasing any \(w_e\) can only decrease every \(R_{\rm eff}\) (Thomson: the same flows, cheaper energy).
  • Series: \(R_{\rm eff}=\sum r_e\) on a path. Parallel: \(1/R_{\rm eff}=\sum w_e\) on parallel edges.

~2 min. Series and parallel rules, and the figure: three parallel two-edge paths give 2/3 (two in series, divided by three in parallel); five series links plus one doubled link give 4 + 1/2.

Foster’s theorem and leverage scores

Foster’s theorem. \(\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}w_e\,R_{\rm eff}(e)=n-1.\)

\(\sum_ew_e\,b_e^\top L^+b_e=\operatorname{tr}\big(L^+\sum_ew_eb_eb_e^\top\big)=\operatorname{tr}(L^+L)=\operatorname{rank}L=n-1\). \(\blacksquare\)

\(w_eR_{\rm eff}(e)\in(0,1]\) is the leverage score of \(e\): the fraction of the unit \(u\)–\(v\) flow that uses the edge \(e\) itself.

Next lecture: sampling edges with probability \(\propto w_eR_{\rm eff}(e)\) gives spectral sparsifiers.

~4 min. Leverage: the electrical u–v flow through e itself is w_e (x_u − x_v) = w_e R_eff(e). So w_e R_eff(e) = 1 iff e is a bridge. Foster checked numerically on a weighted random 3-regular graph (scripts §1).

Random walks: hitting times

Random walk \(P=D^{-1}A\); \(\ h(u,t)\) = expected time to hit \(t\) from \(u\); \(\ \mathrm{vol}(G)=\sum_ud_u=2\sum_ew_e\).

Hitting times are one Laplacian solve. \(h(\cdot,t)\) is the solution, normalized by \(h(t,t)=0\), of \[Lh=d-\mathrm{vol}(G)\,\mathbf 1_t .\]

First-step analysis: \(h(u)=1+\sum_vP_{uv}h(v)\) for \(u\neq t\), i.e. \((Lh)(u)=d_u\). Since \(\mathbf 1^\top Lh=0\), \((Lh)(t)=-\sum_{u\ne t}d_u=d_t-\mathrm{vol}(G)\). \(\blacksquare\)

~2 min. First-step analysis; the right-hand side d − vol·1_t has zero sum, as it must.

Commute times

Recall \(h(\cdot,t)\) solves \(Lh=d-\mathrm{vol}(G)\,\mathbf 1_t\), and \(\mathrm{vol}(G)=\sum_ud_u\).

Commute time identity (Chandra–Raghavan–Ruzzo–Smolensky–Tiwari 1989). \(\ h(s,t)+h(t,s)=\mathrm{vol}(G)\,R_{\rm eff}(s,t).\)

\(h(s,t)+h(t,s)=(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t)^\top L^+\big[(d-\mathrm{vol}\,\mathbf 1_t)-(d-\mathrm{vol}\,\mathbf 1_s)\big]=\mathrm{vol}\cdot(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t)^\top L^+(\mathbf 1_s-\mathbf 1_t)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Path: \(R_{\rm eff}(1,n)=n-1\), so the commute time is \(2(n-1)^2\): diffusive, as in Lecture 3.

~2 min. The two solves differ by vol·(1_s − 1_t), so the d terms cancel. Checked: weighted random 3-regular graph on 40 vertices, h(s,t)+h(t,s) = vol·R_eff = 236.554 (scripts §1). Consequence (CRRST): cover time ≤ 2m·n for unweighted graphs via a spanning tree, and ≥ m·max R_eff.

Why a fast solver matters

One Laplacian solve is a primitive inside many algorithms:

  • Semi-supervised learning / harmonic extension (Zhu–Ghahramani–Lafferty 2003): labels on \(S\), extend by \(Lx=0\) on \(V\setminus S\): a an SDD system (a Laplacian plus a nonnegative diagonal) on the unlabeled vertices.
  • Effective resistances of all edges, approximately, with \(O(\log n/\varepsilon^2)\) solves (Spielman–Srivastava 2008, via Johnson–Lindenstrauss) → spectral sparsification (Lecture 10).
  • Eigenvectors: inverse power iteration \(x\leftarrow L^+x\) finds a vector with Rayleigh quotient \(\le(1+\varepsilon)\lambda_2\) in \(O(\log(n)/\varepsilon)\) solves, at a cost that no longer depends on \(\lambda_2\).
  • Flows: electrical flows inside interior-point methods and multiplicative weights: max flow (Christiano–Kelner–Mądry–Spielman–Teng 2011; Mądry 2013), generalized and min-cost flow (Daitch–Spielman 2008).
  • Sampling: random spanning trees (Kelner–Mądry 2009; Mądry–Straszak–Tarnawski 2015).

The Laplacian paradigm (Teng 2010): reduce a graph problem to a sequence of Laplacian solves, then use a nearly-linear-time solver.

Inverse power iteration: the Fiedler vector in \(O(\log n/\varepsilon)\) solves

Work on \(\mathbf 1^\perp\), where \(L\) is invertible with eigenpairs \((\lambda_i,v_i)\), \(0<\lambda_2\le\dots\le\lambda_n\). Pick a random \(x_0\perp\mathbf 1\) and iterate \[x_k=L^+x_{k-1}=L^{+k}x_0 .\]

  • Component along \(v_i\) is multiplied by \(\lambda_i^{-1}\) per step: the slow modes of \(L\) are the fast modes of \(L^+\).
  • Power iteration needs \(\approx\lambda_n/\lambda_2\) matvecs; here the number of steps does not depend on \(\lambda_n/\lambda_2\), and each step is one solve.

Theorem. With \(x_0\) random, \(R(x_k)=\dfrac{x_k^\top Lx_k}{x_k^\top x_k}\le(1+\varepsilon)\lambda_2\) after \(k=O(\log n/\varepsilon)\) solves.

Let \(a_i=\langle x_0,v_i\rangle\) and \(N_s=\sum_ia_i^2\lambda_i^{-s}\), so \(R(x_k)=N_{2k-1}/N_{2k}\). By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(s\mapsto\log N_s\) is convex, so the ratios \(N_{s-1}/N_s\) are nonincreasing in \(s\). The last of the \(2k\) ratios is at most their geometric mean: \[R(x_k)\le\Big(\frac{N_0}{N_{2k}}\Big)^{1/2k}\le\lambda_2\Big(\frac{\|x_0\|^2}{a_2^2}\Big)^{1/2k}\le\lambda_2\,n^{1/2k}\quad\text{when }a_2^2\ge\|x_0\|^2/n .\] This holds with constant probability for a random \(x_0\), and \(n^{1/2k}\le1+\varepsilon\) once \(k\ge\ln n/(2\ln(1+\varepsilon))\). \(\blacksquare\)

~4 min. The solves are approximate: an ε’-approximate solve at each step with ε’ = 1/poly(n) suffices, which costs an extra log n factor inside the solver, not in the number of solves. The proof: the Rayleigh quotient is a weighted average of eigenvalues, and the weights concentrate geometrically on small ones. Random x_0 = Gaussian projected onto 1^⊥; a_2² ≥ ‖x_0‖²/n with constant probability (anti-concentration of a Gaussian in one coordinate).

  • Path on \(n=300\) vertices: \(\lambda_2\approx10^{-4}\), \(\lambda_n\approx4\).
  • Reaching \(1\%\) error: inverse power iteration takes 3 solves, Lanczos about 190 matvecs, and power iteration over \(3000\) matvecs (still unfinished).
  • A solve costs \(\tilde O(m)\) regardless of \(\lambda_2\), so the total cost no longer depends on how good the cut is.

Classical methods

Gradient descent is diffusion

Minimize \(f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Lx-b^\top x\). Let \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_u)\) be the degree matrix and \(P=D^{-1}A\), so \(D^{-1}L=I-P\).

  • Steepest descent in the \(D\)-norm \(\|x\|_D^2=\sum_ud_ux_u^2\): the gradient is \(\nabla_Df(x)=D^{-1}\nabla f(x)=D^{-1}(Lx-b)\). Since \(L\preceq2D\), the step size \(\eta=\tfrac12\) is safe: \[x_{k+1}=x_k-\tfrac12D^{-1}(Lx_k-b)=\underbrace{\tfrac12(I+P)\,x_k}_{\text{lazy walk step}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12D^{-1}b}_{\text{input charge}} .\]
  • Vertex by vertex: \(\ x_{k+1}(u)=\tfrac12x_k(u)+\tfrac12\,\mathrm{avg}_{v\sim u}\,x_k(v)+\dfrac{b_u}{2d_u}\). Each step diffuses the heat \(x_k\) one lazy step and injects heat \(b_u/2d_u\) at every vertex (\(b_u<0\): a sink).
  • Fixed point: \(x=Px+D^{-1}b\iff Lx=b\). The steady state of diffusion with a constant source is the solution.

The error \(x_k-x^*\) obeys \(x_{k+1}-x^*=\tfrac12(I+P)(x_k-x^*)\): pure heat diffusion, with no source. Gradient descent on \(Lx=b\) is the diffusion of Lectures 3–4 with the charge \(b\) pumped in at every step.

  • Rate: \(\tfrac12(I+P)\) has eigenvalues \(1-\tilde\lambda_i/2\), with \(\tilde\lambda_i\) those of the normalized Laplacian, so \(\ \|x_k-x^*\|_L\le(1-\tilde\lambda_2/2)^k\|x_0-x^*\|_L\). The iteration count is the relaxation time \(2/\tilde\lambda_2\) times \(\ln(1/\varepsilon)\), one product with \(L\) (\(O(m)\)) per step.

~4 min. Heat equation picture: x is temperature, ẋ = −D^{-1}Lx + D^{-1}b is the continuous-time version. Self-adjointness: (I+P)/2 is self-adjoint for both the D-inner product and the L-inner product, which is why the contraction holds in the L-norm on 1^⊥. On a d-regular graph D = dI, so the D-gradient step with η = 1/2 is the Euclidean step with η = 1/(2d); the Euclidean step with η = 1/λ_n is at least as good, since λ_n ≤ 2d.

How many iterations does gradient descent need?

Gradient descent with the Euclidean gradient and \(\eta=1/\lambda_n\) contracts by \(1-1/\kappa\) per step, \(\kappa=\lambda_n/\lambda_2\); on a \(d\)-regular graph \(\kappa\le2d/\lambda_2=2/\tilde\lambda_2\), so it is at least as fast as the diffusion above. So \(\kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)\) iterations, each one product with \(L\): \(O(m)\).

Acceleration: Krylov methods

Start from \(x_0=0\). After \(k\) products with \(L\), any method that combines them outputs \(x_k\in\mathcal K_k=\operatorname{span}\{b,Lb,\dots,L^{k-1}b\}\). Then \(x_k-x^*=q(L)(x_0-x^*)\) for a polynomial \(q\) of degree \(\le k\) with \(q(0)=1\), so \[\|x_k-x^*\|_L\ \le\ \max_{\lambda\in[\lambda_2,\lambda_n]}|q(\lambda)|\cdot\|x_0-x^*\|_L .\]

Chebyshev. The best such \(q\) is a rescaled Chebyshev polynomial, with \[\max_{[\lambda_2,\lambda_n]}|q|\ \le\ 2\Big(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\Big)^k\le2e^{-2k/\sqrt\kappa}.\] Conjugate gradient minimizes \(\|x_k-x^*\|_L\) over \(\mathcal K_k\), so it does at least this well, without knowing \(\lambda_2,\lambda_n\).

The limit of Krylov methods: locality

\(L\) is local: if \(b\) is supported on \(S\), then \(L^jb\) is supported on the \(j\)-neighborhood of \(S\). So \(x_k\) vanishes outside the \((k-1)\)-neighborhood of \(\operatorname{supp}(b)\).

  • Path, \(b=\mathbf 1_1-\mathbf 1_n\): \(x^*\) is linear along the path, nonzero at every vertex (\(n\) even). Every Krylov method needs \(k\ge n/2\), and \(\sqrt\kappa\approx2n/\pi\).
  • Measured (\(n=200\)): CG error \(0.071\) after 99 iterations, \(<10^{-12}\) after 100.

Lower bound. For a worst-case \(b\) and constant \(\varepsilon\), any method that uses \(L\) only through matrix–vector products (started at \(0\)) needs \(\Omega(\text{diameter})\) products. For the path and the grid this is \(\Omega(\sqrt\kappa)\), so total work \(\Omega(n^2)\) and \(\Omega(n^{1.5})\).

The bad case for locality is a path, but a path can be solved directly in \(O(n)\) time by a forward–backward iteration:

  • Forward sweep (eliminate from one end): the flow on edge \(i\) is the partial sum \(f_i=\sum_{j\le i}b_j\).
  • Backward sweep (Ohm’s law): \(x_{i+1}=x_i-r_if_i\), then subtract the mean.

No fill-in, no dependence on \(\kappa\): information crosses the whole path in two passes. The same idea works on any spanning tree.

~3 min. The lower bound is information-theoretic, not about CG specifically. The way out on the next slides: preconditioners that can be inverted cheaply but are global. The path solve is the Thomas algorithm for a tridiagonal system; the tree solve is the same Gaussian elimination from the leaves: O(n), no fill.

Preconditioning

Preconditioned CG. If \(\ L_H\preceq L_G\preceq\kappa\,L_H\ \) (same kernel \(\mathbf 1\)), PCG finds an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate solution of \(L_Gx=b\) in \(O(\sqrt\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))\) iterations. Each iteration: one product with \(L_G\) and one solve with \(L_H\).

  • Why: CG on \(L_H^{+/2}L_GL_H^{+/2}\) (eigenvalues in \([1,\kappa]\)), rewritten so that only \(L_H^+\) is needed.
  • Trade-off: \(H\) must be close to \(G\) (small \(\kappa\)) and easy to solve. \(H=G\) has \(\kappa=1\) and is useless; Jacobi (scaling by \(D\)) is easy and only fixes uneven degrees.
  • Recursion: \(L_H\) only needs to be solved approximately, so it can be solved by PCG with a preconditioner of its own. This is the structure of Spielman–Teng.

Tree preconditioners and stretch

Spanning tree \(T\). For \(e=(u,v)\), let \(P_T(e)\) be the tree path from \(u\) to \(v\) and \(R_T(u,v)=\sum_{t\in P_T(e)}r_t\).

Stretch: \(\ \mathrm{st}_T(e)=\dfrac{R_T(u,v)}{r_e}=w_e\sum_{t\in P_T(e)}r_t\ \) (unweighted: the length of the tree path). \(\ \mathrm{st}_T(G)=\sum_{e\in E}\mathrm{st}_T(e)\).

Lemma (Boman–Hendrickson 2003). \(\ L_T\preceq L_G\preceq\mathrm{st}_T(G)\,L_T.\)

\(L_T\preceq L_G\): \(T\) is a subgraph. For the other side, Cauchy–Schwarz along the path, exactly as for canonical paths with weighted lengths in Lecture 5: \[w_e(x_u-x_v)^2=w_e\Big(\sum_{t\in P_T(e)}\sqrt{r_t}\cdot\frac{\Delta_tx}{\sqrt{r_t}}\Big)^2\le w_eR_T(u,v)\sum_{t\in P_T(e)}\frac{(\Delta_tx)^2}{r_t}\le\mathrm{st}_T(e)\;x^\top L_Tx .\] Sum over \(e\). \(\blacksquare\)

Randomized coordinate descent

Coordinate descent on a quadratic

\(F(z)=\tfrac12z^\top Mz-p^\top z\), \(\ M\succ0\) on \(\mathbb R^N\); \(\ \mu=\lambda_{\min}(M)\); \(\ z^*=M^{-1}p\).

Randomized coordinate descent (RCD). Pick \(i\) with probability \(\ \pi_i=M_{ii}/\operatorname{tr}M\), and minimize \(F\) exactly along \(e_i\): \[z\ \leftarrow\ z-\frac{\nabla_iF(z)}{M_{ii}}\,e_i .\]

Theorem (Leventhal–Lewis 2010; Nesterov 2012). \(\ \ \mathbb E\big[F(z_{k+1})-F^*\,\big|\,z_k\big]\ \le\ \Big(1-\dfrac{\mu}{\operatorname{tr}M}\Big)\big(F(z_k)-F^*\big).\)

One step. \(F\) restricted to the line \(z+se_i\) is \(F(z)+s\nabla_iF+\tfrac{s^2}2M_{ii}\), minimized at \(s=-\nabla_iF/M_{ii}\), with decrease \(\dfrac{(\nabla_iF)^2}{2M_{ii}}\).

Average. \(\ \mathbb E[\text{decrease}]=\sum_i\dfrac{M_{ii}}{\operatorname{tr}M}\cdot\dfrac{(\nabla_iF)^2}{2M_{ii}}=\dfrac{\|\nabla F\|^2}{2\operatorname{tr}M}.\)

Strong convexity. \(\ \|\nabla F\|^2=\|M(z-z^*)\|^2\ge\mu\,(z-z^*)^\top M(z-z^*)=2\mu\,(F(z)-F^*)\). \(\blacksquare\)

~5 min. The whole proof is three lines; take your time with it, everything else today is bookkeeping around it. The sampling probabilities are chosen so that the M_ii cancel. Checked exactly (expectation computed by enumerating coordinates) on 300 random 8×8 instances: the ratio actual/bound never exceeds 1 (scripts §5).

Reading the theorem

  • Compare with GD: rate \(1-\mu/\lambda_{\max}\) per step, each step costs a full product with \(M\). RCD: rate \(1-\mu/\operatorname{tr}M\), each step costs one column. Since \(\operatorname{tr}M\le N\lambda_{\max}\), \(N\) RCD steps are essentially never worse than one GD step (\((1-\beta/N)^N\approx e^{-\beta}\) vs. \(1-\beta\)), and are much better when the diagonal is uneven.
  • Coordinates are a choice. Exact coordinate minimization does not depend on how coordinates are scaled, but the theorem does: with \(z=Sy\) for diagonal \(S\), both \(\mu\) and \(\operatorname{tr}M\) change. Choosing the scaling = choosing the sampling distribution.
  • Special cases: randomized Kaczmarz (Strohmer–Vershynin 2009) is RCD on the dual of the least-norm problem \(\min\|x\|^2\) s.t. \(Ax=b\).
  • General smooth convex \(F\) (Nesterov 2012): coordinate smoothness \(L_i\), sample \(\propto L_i\), step \(-\nabla_iF/L_i\): same rate \(1-\mu/\sum_iL_i\).
  • Acceleration (Nesterov 2012; Lee–Sidford 2013): \(O\big(\sqrt{N\sum_iL_i/\mu}\,\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)\) iterations, each as cheap as a plain one (Lee–Sidford). With sampling \(\propto\sqrt{L_i}\): \(O\big(\sum_i\sqrt{L_i/\mu}\,\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)\) (Allen-Zhu–Qu–Richtárik–Yuan 2016).

~3 min. The point to emphasize: when we apply RCD to flows, the only real decision is the choice of coordinates (the tree) and their scaling (by √r_e). Everything else follows from the theorem.

KOSZ: cycle toggling is coordinate descent

The cycle space

Fix a spanning tree \(T\). Every off-tree edge \(e=(a,b)\) closes a fundamental cycle \(C_e\): \(e\) from \(a\) to \(b\), then the tree path from \(b\) back to \(a\).

\(c_e\in\{0,\pm1\}^E\): the unit circulation around \(C_e\), so \(B^\top c_e=0\) and \(c_e(e)=1\).

Lemma. Let \(f_0\) be the unique flow on \(T\) routing \(\chi\). The flows routing \(\chi\) are exactly \[f=f_0+Cy=f_0+\sum_{e\notin T}y_e\,c_e,\qquad y\in\mathbb R^{E\setminus T},\] and \(y_e=f_e\) for every off-tree \(e\).

\(f-f_0\) is a circulation. Subtract \(\sum_{e\notin T}f_ec_e\): what is left is a circulation supported on the tree, hence \(0\). \(\blacksquare\)

~3 min. C has full column rank m − n + 1: its rows indexed by off-tree edges form the identity. This is the “coordinate system” the tree gives us.

Energy in cycle coordinates

\[F(y)=\mathcal E(f_0+Cy),\qquad \nabla^2F=C^\top RC .\]

  • Gradient = Kirchhoff’s voltage law violation. \[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_e}=c_e^\top Rf=\sum_{t\in C_e}c_e(t)\,r_tf_t=:\Delta_e(f),\] the total potential drop around \(C_e\). \(\ \Delta_e(f)=0\) for all \(e\notin T\) iff \(f\) is electrical.
  • Diagonal = cycle resistance: \(\ (C^\top RC)_{ee}=\sum_{t\in C_e}r_t=r_e+R_T(a,b)=:R_e\).
  • Exact coordinate step = cycle toggle: \[f\ \leftarrow\ f-\frac{\Delta_e(f)}{R_e}\,c_e\qquad\text{(after which }\Delta_e(f)=0\text{)}.\]

Physical picture. Pick a cycle; push just enough flow around it that the potential drops around it sum to zero. KCL holds throughout; we repair KVL one cycle at a time.

~3 min. This algorithm is the “cycle toggling” of KOSZ; it predates them as a heuristic (it’s a Gauss–Seidel / Kaczmarz method on the cycle space), but without the choice of tree, scaling and sampling it has no nearly-linear guarantee.

The right scaling: \(z_e=\sqrt{r_e}\,y_e\)

In the variables \(z_e=\sqrt{r_e}\,y_e\) the Hessian is \(\ M=R_{\rm off}^{-1/2}\,C^\top RC\,R_{\rm off}^{-1/2}\), with \(R_{\rm off}=\operatorname{diag}(r_e)_{e\notin T}\).

Lemma. (1) \(\ M_{ee}=\dfrac{R_e}{r_e}=1+\mathrm{st}_T(e)\). \(\quad\) (2) \(\ \lambda_{\min}(M)\ge1\). \(\quad\) (3) \(\ \operatorname{tr}M=\tau(T):=\displaystyle\sum_{e\notin T}\big(1+\mathrm{st}_T(e)\big)\le m+\mathrm{st}_T(G)\).

  1. \((Cy)_e=y_e\) on off-tree edges, so \(\ z^\top Mz=\sum_{t\in E}r_t(Cy)_t^2\ \ge\ \sum_{e\notin T}r_ey_e^2=\|z\|^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary (RCD on flows). Sample \(e\notin T\) with probability \(\ \dfrac{R_e/r_e}{\tau(T)}\) and toggle \(C_e\). Then \[\mathbb E\big[\mathcal E(f_{k})-\mathcal E^*\big]\ \le\ \Big(1-\frac1{\tau(T)}\Big)^{k}\big(\mathcal E(f_0)-\mathcal E^*\big).\]

  • Without rescaling (coordinates \(y\), sampling \(\propto R_e\)) the theorem gives \(\mu\ge\min_er_e\) and \(\operatorname{tr}=\sum_eR_e\): a rate that depends on the spread of the weights.

~4 min. Here st_T(e) = R_T(a,b)/r_e: for unit weights y = z and nothing changes. (3): the tree edges each have stretch 1 and are not coordinates, so τ(T) = st_T(G) + m − 2(n − 1) exactly; we only need the upper bound. The rescaling is “measure the flow on e in units of √r_e”, i.e. use the energy norm restricted to the off-tree edges; λ_min ≥ 1 says the full energy dominates the off-tree energy.

Optimality conditions and the geometry of a toggle

Optimality conditions for \(\min\{\mathcal E(f):B^\top f=\chi\}\):

  • KCL (feasibility) \(\ B^\top f=\chi\): every iterate satisfies it.
  • KVL / Ohm (stationarity) \(\ Rf=Bx\) for some \(x\) \(\iff\) \(Rf\perp\ker B^\top=\) cycle space \(\iff\) \(\Delta_e(f)=\langle c_e,f\rangle_R=0\) for all \(e\notin T\).

The gap is the violation. The tree potentials satisfy Ohm exactly on \(T\), and the Ohm residual \(r_ef_e-b_e^\top x_T\) equals \(\Delta_e(f)\) on \(e\notin T\). By the duality gap identity (Electrical flows slide), \[\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal D(x_T)=\frac12\sum_{e\notin T}\frac{\Delta_e(f)^2}{r_e}.\]

Geometry, in the energy inner product \(\langle f,g\rangle_R=\sum_er_ef_eg_e\) (so \(\|c_e\|_R^2=R_e\)):

  • \(f^*\) is the minimum-norm point of the affine space \(f_0+\mathrm{Cyc}\), so \(f^*\perp_R\mathrm{Cyc}\) and \(\ \mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E^*=\tfrac12\|f-f^*\|_R^2\).
  • The KVL hyperplanes \(H_e=\{g:\langle c_e,g\rangle_R=0\}\) all contain \(f^*\), and \(\Delta_e(f)=\langle c_e,f-f^*\rangle_R\).
  • A toggle is the \(R\)-orthogonal projection onto \(H_e\). By Pythagoras, \(\ \tfrac12\|f'-f^*\|_R^2=\tfrac12\|f-f^*\|_R^2-\dfrac{\Delta_e^2}{2R_e}\): randomized Kaczmarz in the energy geometry.

Expected progress = gap / \(\tau\). With \(p_e=\dfrac{R_e/r_e}{\tau(T)}\), \[\mathbb E\big[\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E(f')\big]=\sum_{e\notin T}\frac{R_e}{r_e\tau}\cdot\frac{\Delta_e^2}{2R_e}=\frac{\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal D(x_T)}{\tau}.\] The gap is sandwiched: \(\quad\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E^*\ \le\ \mathcal E(f)-\mathcal D(x_T)\ \le\ \tau\,\big(\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E^*\big)\).

  • Lower half = weak duality, \(\mathcal D(x_T)\le\mathcal D(x^*)=\mathcal E^*\). With the progress identity it gives \(\mathbb E[\mathcal E(f')-\mathcal E^*]\le(1-\frac1\tau)(\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E^*)\): a second proof of the corollary, with no eigenvalues.
  • Upper half = smoothness, \(\lambda_{\max}(M)\le\tau\) (proved in the Stop lemma below).

The two halves are exactly \(\lambda_{\min}(M)\ge1\) and \(\lambda_{\max}(M)\le\operatorname{tr}M=\tau\). Equivalently, the rescaled cycles \(c_e/\sqrt{r_e}\) form a frame for the cycle space with frame bounds \([1,\tau]\). The tree supplies a dual certificate whose gap is the squared gradient, so weak duality is the strong-convexity (Polyak–Łojasiewicz) inequality.

~5 min. Optional but recommended: it explains why the sampling distribution is what it is. p_e ∝ R_e/r_e cancels the R_e in the projection’s decrease and leaves each violation weighted by its share of the gap. Equivalence with the eigenvalue statement: with d = z − z, F − F = ½ dᵀMd and the gap is ½‖∇F‖² = ½ dᵀM²d. So “gap ≥ F − F* for all d” ⟺ dᵀM²d ≥ dᵀMd ⟺ every eigenvalue λ > 0 of M has λ² ≥ λ ⟺ λ_min ≥ 1. This progress-vs-gap argument is essentially KOSZ’s original analysis; the coordinate-descent and Kaczmarz views (Lee–Sidford; Strohmer–Vershynin with frame bounds) prove the same two inequalities. Note x_T is not the best dual for f: minimizing the Ohm residual over x gives x*. It is the cheapest certificate, and it is enough.

The algorithm

SimpleSolver (KOSZ 2013). Input \(G\), demand \(\chi\), accuracy \(\varepsilon\).

  1. Compute a low-stretch spanning tree \(T\); the tree flow \(f_0\); \(R_e\) and \(p_e=\dfrac{R_e/r_e}{\tau(T)}\) for \(e\notin T\).
  2. Repeat \(K=\big\lceil\tau(T)\ln\big(\tau(T)\,\mathrm{st}_T(G)/\varepsilon\big)\big\rceil\) times: sample \(e\sim p\), compute \(\Delta_e(f)\), set \(f\leftarrow f-\dfrac{\Delta_e(f)}{R_e}c_e\).
  3. Return the tree-induced potentials \(x_T(f)\): \(x_T(u)-x_T(v)=r_tf_t\) on every tree edge \(t=(u,v)\).

Two things remain to prove:

  • Start: \(f_0\) is not too far from optimal.
  • Stop: a flow with small energy gap gives potentials with small \(L\)-norm error.

And one thing remains to build:

  • Each toggle in \(O(\log n)\) time, although the cycle \(C_e\) has \(|P_T(e)|+1\) edges (for unit weights, \(\mathrm{st}_T(e)+1\)).

~2 min. Steps 1 and 3 are O(m log n log log n) (Abraham–Neiman) and O(n). The output is a vector of potentials, which is what “solve Lx = χ” asks for; the flow is a by-product.

Start: the tree flow

Lemma. \(\ \mathcal E(f_0)\le\mathrm{st}_T(G)\cdot\mathcal E^*\).

\(f_0\) is the only flow on \(T\) routing \(\chi\), so it is the electrical flow of the tree, and by Thomson \(\mathcal E(f_0)=\tfrac12\chi^\top L_T^+\chi\). By Boman–Hendrickson, \(L_G\preceq s\,L_T\) with \(s=\mathrm{st}_T(G)\), and both have kernel \(\mathbf 1\), so \(L_T^+\preceq s\,L_G^+\).

Why inversion reverses \(\preceq\): convex conjugates. For \(A\succeq0\) with kernel \(\mathbf 1\) and \(\chi\perp\mathbf 1\), the conjugate of \(\phi_A(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Ax\) is \[\phi_A^*(\chi)=\sup_x\big(\chi^\top x-\tfrac12x^\top Ax\big)=\tfrac12\chi^\top A^+\chi\qquad(\text{attained at }x=A^+\chi).\] Conjugation reverses pointwise order: \(\phi_{L_G}\le\phi_{sL_T}\) gives \(\phi_{L_G}^*\ge\phi_{sL_T}^*\), i.e. \[\mathcal E^*=\tfrac12\chi^\top L_G^+\chi\ \ge\ \tfrac12\chi^\top(sL_T)^+\chi=\frac1s\,\tfrac12\chi^\top L_T^+\chi=\frac{\mathcal E(f_0)}{s}.\ \blacksquare\]

  • The same lemma that made \(T\) a preconditioner now bounds the starting error. It enters only through a logarithm.
  • Measured (\(10\times10\) grid, random weights, BFS tree): \(\mathcal E(f_0)/\mathcal E^*=8.2\), against \(\mathrm{st}_T(G)=1\,777\).

~3 min. The conjugate here is exactly the dual objective of Thomson’s principle: φ*_L(χ) = max_x D(x) = E*. So the step reads physically: more conductance (L_G ⪰ L_T/s) lowers the dual objective at every set of potentials, hence lowers its maximum, the energy. The same-kernel hypothesis is needed: for χ ⊥ 1 the sup is finite and attained, and constants in x do not matter. Without it the claim fails (A = diag(1,0) ⪯ B = diag(1,ε), but B⁺ ⋠ A⁺). Numbers: scripts §6.

Stop: from flows to potentials

Lemma. Let \(x_T=x_T(f)\) be the tree-induced potentials of a flow \(f\) routing \(\chi\). Then \[\|x_T-x^*\|_L^2\ \le\ 2\big(\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal D(x_T)\big)\ =\ \sum_{e\notin T}\frac{\Delta_e(f)^2}{r_e}\ =\ \|\nabla_zF\|^2\ \le\ 2\tau(T)\,\big(\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E^*\big).\]

First inequality: \(\ \|x_T-x^*\|_L^2=2\big(\mathcal D(x^*)-\mathcal D(x_T)\big)=2\big(\mathcal E^*-\mathcal D(x_T)\big)\le2\big(\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal D(x_T)\big)\).

Equality: the boxed duality gap, times \(2\). On tree edges \(r_tf_t=b_t^\top x_T\), so those terms vanish. On \(e=(a,b)\notin T\), \(\ r_ef_e-b_e^\top x_T=r_ef_e+\big(x_T(b)-x_T(a)\big)=\Delta_e(f)\), the drop around \(C_e\).

Gradient: \(\ \partial F/\partial z_e=\Delta_e/\sqrt{r_e}\).

Last inequality: for a quadratic, \(\ \|\nabla F\|^2=\|M(z-z^*)\|^2\le2\lambda_{\max}(M)(F-F^*)\), with \(\lambda_{\max}(M)\le\operatorname{tr}M=\tau(T)\) and \(F=\mathcal E\). \(\blacksquare\)

The duality gap of the tree potentials is (half) the squared gradient. Smoothness (\(\lambda_{\max}(M)\le\tau\), the upper half of the sandwich) turns small energy suboptimality into small KVL violations, and weak duality turns small violations into accurate potentials.

~4 min. Checked: on a weighted 10×10 grid after 300 toggles, 2(E(f) − D(x_T)) = Σ Δ_e²/r_e = 10.7939 to 12 digits, and ‖x_T − x*‖²_L = 9.37 (scripts §6). The τ in the last inequality is a trace bound for λ_max; it only costs a log τ in the iteration count.

The main theorem

Three ingredients, with \(\tau=\tau(T)\) and \(\mathcal E^*=\tfrac12\|x^*\|_L^2\) the optimal energy:

Start. \(\ \mathcal E(f_0)\le\mathrm{st}_T(G)\cdot\mathcal E^*\).

RCD. Toggling a cycle \(C_e\) sampled with probability \(\propto R_e/r_e\) gives \(\ \mathbb E[\mathcal E(f_k)-\mathcal E^*]\le\big(1-\tfrac1\tau\big)^k\big(\mathcal E(f_0)-\mathcal E^*\big)\).

Stop. For the tree-induced potentials \(x_T\) of any flow \(f\) routing \(\chi\): \(\ \|x_T-x^*\|_L^2\le2\tau\,\big(\mathcal E(f)-\mathcal E^*\big)\).

Theorem (KOSZ 2013). After \(K\ge\tau(T)\ln\dfrac{\tau(T)\,\mathrm{st}_T(G)}{\varepsilon}\) toggles, \(\ \mathbb E\,\|x_T(f_K)-x^*\|_L^2\le\varepsilon\,\|x^*\|_L^2\). (For an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate solution in the sense of the definition, replace \(\varepsilon\) by \(\varepsilon^2\): same order of \(K\).)

Chain the three lemmas (stop, then RCD, then start): \[\mathbb E\|x_T-x^*\|_L^2\ \overset{\rm stop}\le\ 2\tau\,\mathbb E[\mathcal E(f_K)-\mathcal E^*]\ \overset{\rm RCD}\le\ 2\tau\,e^{-K/\tau}\big(\mathcal E(f_0)-\mathcal E^*\big)\ \overset{\rm start}\le\ \tau\,e^{-K/\tau}\,\mathrm{st}_T(G)\,\|x^*\|_L^2\le\varepsilon\,\|x^*\|_L^2.\ \blacksquare\]

Running time. With Abraham–Neiman, \(\tau(T)\le m+\mathrm{st}_T(G)=O(m\log n\log\log n)\), and \(\log(\tau\,\mathrm{st}_T(G)/\varepsilon)=O(\log(n/\varepsilon))\). With \(O(\log n)\) per toggle: \[O\big(m\log^2n\,\log\log n\,\log(n/\varepsilon)\big).\]

  • No recursion, no sparsifiers, no Chebyshev: a tree, a sampling distribution and a data structure.
  • Acceleration (Lee–Sidford 2013): accelerated RCD with \(\mu\ge1\) needs \(O\big(\sqrt{N\operatorname{tr}M}\log(\cdot)\big)=O\big(\sqrt{m\,\tau(T)}\log(\cdot)\big)\) toggles: \(\tilde O(m\log^{3/2}n)\).

~4 min. Expectation → high probability: Markov plus O(log 1/δ) independent repetitions, keeping the run with the smallest computable gap E(f) − D(x_T) (an upper bound on the error). On the accelerated version: the iteration count drops from τ = Õ(m log n) to √(mτ) = Õ(m √log n); the hard part of Lee–Sidford is implementing the accelerated step (which touches two or three sequences) with the same O(log n) data structure, which they do by maintaining the iterates in a changing basis. Their bound is O(m log^{3/2} n √(log log n) log(n/ε))-type; check their paper for the exact log(1/ε) term.

The data structure

What a toggle needs

A toggle of \(e=(a,b)\) reads one number and writes one push:

  • read \(\ \Delta_e=r_ef_e+x_T(b)-x_T(a)\): only the potentials of the two endpoints of \(e\);
  • write \(\ f_e\leftarrow f_e+\alpha\) and push \(\alpha\) through \(T\) from \(b\) to \(a\). Stored naively, this changes \(f_t\) on every edge of the tree path: \(O(|P_T(e)|)\).

Never store the tree flows. The off-tree flows determine the tree flow: it is the unique tree flow routing \(\chi+q\), where \(q\) is the net demand the off-tree flows inject into \(T\). Pushing \(\alpha\) from \(b\) to \(a\) changes \(q\) at two vertices only: \(\ q_b\mathrel{+}=\alpha,\ q_a\mathrel{-}=\alpha\). The cost moves from the write to the read: we must recover \(x_T(b)-x_T(a)\) from \(q\).

~2 min. The write is the expensive part if we store tree flows; the idea box says how to avoid it. The next slide draws the state we keep.

Centroid decomposition: \(O(\log n)\) per toggle

  • Read = two queries. The off-tree flows inject charges \(q=\sum_{e=(a,b)\notin T}f_e(\mathbf 1_b-\mathbf 1_a)\) into \(T\), the tree flow routes \(\chi+q\), and with \(R_T\) the resistance metric of \(T\), \[x_T(u)=x_0(u)-\tfrac12\sum_vq_v\,R_T(u,v)+\text{const},\qquad x_0=L_T^+\chi .\] The read \(x_T(b)-x_T(a)\) is two charge-weighted sums of tree distances.

  • Centroid tree. Every tree has a vertex whose removal leaves components of size \(\le n/2\). Recursing gives a centroid tree of depth \(\le\log_2n+1\), and if \(c\) is the centroid-tree LCA of \(u,v\), then \(c\) lies on the tree path from \(u\) to \(v\): \[R_T(u,v)=R_T(u,c)+R_T(c,v).\]

  • Store at each centroid the total charge and the charge-weighted distance of its component. An update \(q_v\mathrel{+}=\alpha\) and a query at \(u\) both walk the \(\le\log_2n+1\) centroid ancestors.

Each toggle costs \(O(\log n)\) after \(O(n\log n)\) preprocessing. The same structure returns \(R_T(a,b)\), hence the sampling probabilities.

~4 min. The formula for x_T comes from L_T⁺ written in terms of R_T: R_T(u,v) = L⁺_uu + L⁺_vv − 2L⁺_uv, and Σ_v q_v = 0. The key point is the reduction “path update + path query” → “point update + sum of weighted distances”, a classical problem on a static tree. Full details (stored sums S_c, D_c, D↑_c, the query formula and its proof, the centroid-chain picture) are in the appendix at the end of the deck. Takeaways

  1. Electrical flows are the primal, potentials the dual; the gap is \(\tfrac12\sum_e(r_ef_e-b_e^\top x)^2/r_e\). Effective resistance, hitting and commute times are Laplacian solves.
  2. Matrix–vector methods need \(\Omega(\text{diameter})\) products for a worst-case \(b\), which is \(\Omega(\sqrt\kappa)\) on paths and grids: GD is diffusion, Krylov is local.
  3. Trees are global: \(L_T\preceq L_G\preceq\mathrm{st}_T(G)L_T\), and low-stretch trees have \(\mathrm{st}_T(G)=\tilde O(m)\).
  4. RCD on a quadratic: sample \(\propto M_{ii}\), rate \(1-\lambda_{\min}/\operatorname{tr}M\). Three lines.
  5. KOSZ = RCD in the cycle basis of a low-stretch tree, scaled by \(\sqrt{r_e}\): \(\lambda_{\min}\ge1\), \(\operatorname{tr}=\tau(T)=\tilde O(m)\). Plus a start lemma (stretch), a stop lemma (duality gap = gradient) and an \(O(\log n)\) data structure (centroids + resistance distances).

Discussion questions

  1. Run RCD on the dual \(\max_x\mathcal D(x)\), one vertex potential at a time. What is the rate? Why is the primal (cycle space) better?
  2. Where does the proof use that \(T\) is a tree? What changes if \(T\) is replaced by a subgraph \(H\) with a few cycles (an ultra-sparsifier)?
  3. The stop lemma used \(\lambda_{\max}(M)\le\operatorname{tr}M\). What is \(\lambda_{\max}(M)\) on the unit grid with the recursive tree, and does it matter?
  4. KOSZ measures cost in toggles, CG in matrix–vector products. On the unit-weight grid, CG wins by a factor \(>10\). Which part of the KOSZ bound is loose here, and which part is real?

Appendix: the data structure in detail

What we store: off-tree flows and charges

State of the data structure (a \(3\times4\) grid, spanning tree \(T\)):

  • Stored: the off-tree flows \(f_e\) (most are \(0\)) and the charges \(q\in\mathbb R^V\).
  • Not stored: the tree flow. It is the tree flow routing \(\chi+q\).
  • A toggle of \(e=(a,b)\): change \(f_e\) by \(\alpha\) and \(q_b\mathrel{+}=\alpha\), \(q_a\mathrel{-}=\alpha\). Two point updates, no path.
  • A read needs \(x_T(a),x_T(b)\): potentials of the tree flow routing \(\chi+q\).

~2 min. The picture to keep in mind for the rest of this section. Orange arrows are the only flows we remember; each one leaves a +f at its head and a −f at its tail, and q_v is the sum of what lands at v (the vertex with f_1 − f_2 receives from one edge and sends along another). The tree edges carry whatever is needed to route χ + q; we never write them.

Potentials from charges

Lemma (potentials from charges). Let \(q=\sum_{e=(a,b)\notin T}f_e(\mathbf 1_b-\mathbf 1_a)\), the net demand that off-tree flows inject into \(T\). Then, up to an additive constant, \[x_T=x_0-\tfrac12\Psi,\qquad\Psi(u):=\sum_vq_v\,R_T(u,v),\] where \(x_0=L_T^+\chi\) are the potentials of \(f_0\).

The tree flow routes \(\chi+q\), so \(x_T=L_T^+(\chi+q)=x_0+L_T^+q\). For any connected graph, and in particular for \(T\): \(\ R_T(u,v)=(\mathbf 1_u-\mathbf 1_v)^\top L_T^+(\mathbf 1_u-\mathbf 1_v)=L^+_{T,uu}+L^+_{T,vv}-2L^+_{T,uv}\). Multiply by \(q_v\) and sum over \(v\), using \(\sum_vq_v=0\): \[\Psi(u)=\sum_vq_vL^+_{T,vv}-2\,(L_T^+q)(u)=\text{const}-2(L_T^+q)(u).\ \blacksquare\]

So a toggle is: two queries \(\Psi(a),\Psi(b)\) (the read needs \(x_T\) only at \(a\) and \(b\); the constant cancels in \(x_T(b)-x_T(a)\), and every push keeps \(\sum_vq_v=0\)) and two point updates \(q_b\mathrel{+}=\alpha\), \(q_a\mathrel{-}=\alpha\). The next two slides do both in \(O(\log n)\).

~4 min. This is where effective resistance comes back: L⁺ of the tree is determined by the tree’s resistance metric, R_T. The reduction turns “path update + path query” into “point update + sum of weighted distances”, which is a classical data-structure problem on a static tree. (Equivalently, the four-point identity: the signed overlap of the tree paths x→y and a→b is ½[R(x,b) + R(y,a) − R(x,a) − R(y,b)].)

Centroid decomposition

Lemma. Every tree on \(N\) vertices has a centroid: a vertex whose removal leaves components of size \(\le N/2\).

Start anywhere; while some component of \(T-c\) has \(>N/2\) vertices, move \(c\) to its neighbor in it. The component on the other side has \(<N/2\) vertices, so we never step back. \(\blacksquare\)

Remove the centroid \(c\) of \(T\); recurse on each component. This gives the centroid tree: \(c\) is the parent of the centroids of the components of \(K_c-c\), where \(K_c\) is the component in which \(c\) was chosen.

  • Depth \(\le\log_2n+1\), and every vertex is the centroid of exactly one \(K_c\).
  • Key property: if \(c\) is the lowest common ancestor of \(u,v\) in the centroid tree, then \(u,v\in K_c\) but \(c\) separates them (or is one of them), so \[c\in P_T(u,v)\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_T(u,v)=R_T(u,c)+R_T(c,v).\]
  • Precompute \(R_T(u,c)\) for every \(u\) and every centroid ancestor \(c\) of \(u\): \(O(n\log n)\) time and space (one BFS per component).

~3 min. The components K_c are subtrees, so the tree path from u to v stays inside K_c, and it must pass through c because u and v lie in different components of K_c − c. Measured depth: 7 for n = 64, 12 for n = 4096 (log₂ n = 6, 12).

A query along the centroid chain

  • Colours = the sets \(K_{c_i}\setminus K_{c_{i-1}}\): exactly the vertices \(v\) whose centroid-tree LCA with \(u\) is \(c_i\).
  • For \(v\) in the \(i\)-th colour, the tree path from \(u\) to \(v\) passes through \(c_i\), so \(R_T(u,v)=R_T(u,c_i)+R_T(c_i,v)\).
  • The query sums \(q_v\,R_T(u,v)\) one colour at a time, using only the sums stored at the \(\le\log_2n+1\) centroids on the chain (defined on the next slide).

~2 min. Path of 15 vertices, centroids 7, then 3 and 11, then 1, 5, 9, 13. For u = 4 the chain is 4, 5, 3, 7 (4 is the centroid of its own singleton component). A path is the worst case for locality and the easiest to draw; in a general tree the same picture holds, with c_i separating u from K_{c_i} minus K_{c_{i-1}}. Sum of weighted distances in \(O(\log n)\)

For each centroid \(c\) with parent \(\hat c\) in the centroid tree (\(D^\uparrow\) only for \(c\ne\) root), store \[S_c=\sum_{v\in K_c}q_v,\qquad D_c=\sum_{v\in K_c}q_v\,R_T(v,c),\qquad D^\uparrow_c=\sum_{v\in K_c}q_v\,R_T(v,\hat c).\]

Update \(q_v\mathrel{+}=\alpha\): for each centroid ancestor \(c\) of \(v\): \(\ S_c\mathrel{+}=\alpha\), \(\ D_c\mathrel{+}=\alpha R_T(v,c)\), \(\ D^\uparrow_c\mathrel{+}=\alpha R_T(v,\hat c)\).

Query \(\Psi(u)\): with \(u=c_0,c_1,\dots,c_\ell\) the centroid ancestors of \(u\) (bottom-up), \[\Psi(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\Big[\big(D_{c_i}-D^\uparrow_{c_{i-1}}\big)+R_T(u,c_i)\big(S_{c_i}-S_{c_{i-1}}\big)\Big]\qquad(\text{terms with }c_{-1}\text{ are }0).\]

The vertices \(v\in K_{c_i}\setminus K_{c_{i-1}}\) are exactly those whose centroid-tree LCA with \(u\) is \(c_i\). For them \(R_T(u,v)=R_T(u,c_i)+R_T(c_i,v)\). Summing \(q_v\) times this over the set gives the \(i\)-th term, because \(\hat c_{i-1}=c_i\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Both operations touch \(\le\log_2n+1\) centroids: \(O(\log n)\) per toggle. Measured on the \(64\times64\) grid: about 35 array entries per toggle (recursive tree; 39 with the BFS tree).
  • Preprocessing \(O(n\log n)\). The same structure gives each \(R_T(a,b)\), hence \(R_e\) and \(p_e\), in \(O(\log n)\).
