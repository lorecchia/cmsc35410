Linear Algebra Review, Part I

Diagonalization in finite dimensions

Draft. Sections 1 and 6 are the essentials.

This note reviews what it means to diagonalize a linear map on a finite-dimensional space, and what can be said when that is impossible.

  • Section 1 fixes notation.
  • Sections 2–3 cover eigenvalues, the characteristic and minimal polynomials, and exact criteria for diagonalizability, including Cayley–Hamilton.
  • Section 4 proves the Schur triangularization and characterizes the unitarily diagonalizable (normal) matrices.
  • Section 5 develops the Jordan canonical form, the complete answer for non-diagonalizable matrices, together with a warning about its numerical instability.
  • Section 6 proves the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators and derives the tools used throughout the course: Rayleigh quotients, Courant–Fischer, Weyl’s inequality, and self-adjointness with respect to weighted inner products such as the degree inner product of a graph.

Part II extends Section 6 to infinite dimensions, using the proof strategy of Section 6 almost verbatim, so that proof is worth reading carefully. Most of the course needs only Sections 1 and 6. Sections 2–5 matter whenever matrices are not symmetric: directed graphs, non-reversible Markov chains, and iterative methods.

1. Notation and conventions

  • \(\mathbb{F}\) denotes \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\). For \(z \in \mathbb{C}\), \(\bar z\) is the complex conjugate.
  • \(V\) is a finite-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb{F}\). A linear map \(A : V \to V\) is identified, after choosing a basis, with a matrix in \(\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\). Matrices \(A\) and \(S^{-1}AS\) (\(S\) invertible) are similar: they represent the same map in different bases.
  • \(\mathbb{F}[t]\) is the ring of polynomials with coefficients in \(\mathbb{F}\). A polynomial splits over \(\mathbb{F}\) if it is a product of linear factors \((t - \lambda)\) with \(\lambda \in \mathbb{F}\).

The remaining conventions concern inner products, which are needed from Section 4 on.

  • An inner product on an \(\mathbb{F}\)-vector space \(V\) is a map \(\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle : V\times V \to \mathbb{F}\) that is linear in the first argument, satisfies \(\langle x, y\rangle = \overline{\langle y, x\rangle}\), and has \(\langle x, x\rangle > 0\) for \(x \neq 0\). (So it is conjugate-linear in the second argument.) The induced norm is \(\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x\rangle}\).
  • On \(\mathbb{F}^n\) the standard inner product is \(\langle x, y\rangle = \sum_i x_i \bar y_i = y^* x\), where \(y^*\) is the conjugate transpose. Over \(\mathbb{R}\), \(y^* = y^\top\).
  • Two basic identities hold in every inner product space:
    • Cauchy–Schwarz: \(|\langle x, y\rangle| \le \|x\|\,\|y\|\), with equality iff \(x, y\) are linearly dependent.
    • Parallelogram law: \(\|x+y\|^2 + \|x-y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2\).
  • \(x \perp y\) means \(\langle x, y\rangle = 0\). For a set \(S\), \(S^\perp = \{x : x \perp s \text{ for all } s \in S\}\). This is always a (closed) subspace.
  • For a linear map \(A : V \to V\), the adjoint \(A^*\) is the linear map with \(\langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, A^* y\rangle\) for all \(x, y\). For matrices with the standard inner product, \(A^*\) is the conjugate transpose. \(A\) is self-adjoint if \(A = A^*\): a real symmetric matrix, or a complex Hermitian matrix.
  • \(A\) is positive semidefinite (PSD), written \(A \succeq 0\), if it is self-adjoint and \(\langle Ax, x\rangle \ge 0\) for all \(x\). \(A \succeq B\) means \(A - B \succeq 0\).
  • The Rayleigh quotient of a self-adjoint \(A\) is \(R_A(x) = \dfrac{\langle Ax, x\rangle}{\langle x, x\rangle}\) for \(x \ne 0\).

Example (why self-adjointness shows up everywhere in this course). For an undirected graph \(G = (V, E, w)\), the adjacency matrix \(A\), the Laplacian \(L = D - A\), and the normalized Laplacian \(\mathcal{L} = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}\) are all real symmetric. So are covariance matrices \(\frac1m \sum_i x_i x_i^\top\) and kernel (Gram) matrices \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\). The random-walk matrix \(P = D^{-1}A\) and the random-walk Laplacian \(L_{\mathrm{rw}} = D^{-1}L = I - P\) are not symmetric. They are, however, self-adjoint for the degree-weighted inner product \(\langle x, y\rangle_D = y^* D x\), and this is equivalent to the symmetry of \(\mathcal{L}\) (Section 6.5).

2. Eigenvalues and the characteristic polynomial

Throughout Sections 2–5, \(V\) is a vector space over \(\mathbb{F}\) of dimension \(n \ge 1\) and \(A : V \to V\) is linear. No inner product is needed until Section 4. Choosing a basis identifies \(A\) with a matrix in \(\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\). Changing the basis replaces the matrix by a similar one, \(S^{-1}AS\) with \(S\) invertible. Every notion below is invariant under similarity, so it belongs to the linear map, not the matrix.

2.1 Eigenvalues and multiplicities

A scalar \(\lambda \in \mathbb{F}\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if \(Av = \lambda v\) for some \(v \ne 0\), called an eigenvector. The eigenspace is \(E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda I)\). Its dimension \(g_\lambda = \dim E_\lambda\) is the geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda\). The set of eigenvalues is the spectrum \(\sigma(A)\).

The characteristic polynomial is \[ p_A(t) = \det(tI - A) = t^n - (\operatorname{tr} A)\, t^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n \det A . \] It is monic of degree \(n\). It is similarity invariant, since \(\det(tI - S^{-1}AS) = \det\big(S^{-1}(tI - A)S\big) = \det(tI - A)\). And \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue iff \(A - \lambda I\) is singular iff \(p_A(\lambda) = 0\). The multiplicity \(a_\lambda\) of \(\lambda\) as a root of \(p_A\) is its algebraic multiplicity. If \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{F}\), i.e., factors into linear factors \(p_A(t) = \prod_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} (t - \lambda)^{a_\lambda}\), then comparing coefficients gives \[ \operatorname{tr} A = \sum_\lambda a_\lambda \lambda, \qquad \det A = \prod_\lambda \lambda^{a_\lambda}. \]

Existence of eigenvalues depends on the field.

  • Over \(\mathbb{C}\), the fundamental theorem of algebra says \(p_A\) always splits. So every linear map on a nonzero complex vector space has an eigenvalue.
  • Over \(\mathbb{R}\), this fails. The rotation \(R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\) has \(p(t) = t^2 - 2\cos\theta\, t + 1\), with no real roots unless \(\theta \in \pi\mathbb{Z}\). The standard remedy is to regard a real matrix as a complex one. Its eigenvalues are then real or come in conjugate pairs.

Lemma 2.1. Eigenvectors \(v_1, \dots, v_k\) belonging to distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k\) are linearly independent.

Proof. Suppose not, and take a nontrivial relation \(\sum_{i\in I} c_i v_i = 0\) with the fewest nonzero terms. It has at least two terms. Pick \(j \in I\) and apply \(A - \lambda_j I\): this gives \(\sum_{i \in I} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)v_i = 0\). The new relation is nontrivial, since \(\lambda_i \ne \lambda_j\) for \(i \ne j\), and has fewer terms. That contradicts minimality. \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 2.2. For every eigenvalue \(\lambda\), \(1 \le g_\lambda \le a_\lambda\).

Proof. Extend a basis of \(E_\lambda\) to a basis of \(V\). In this basis, \(A = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_{g} & * \\ 0 & C\end{pmatrix}\) with \(g = g_\lambda\). So \(p_A(t) = (t - \lambda)^{g}\, p_C(t)\), and \((t-\lambda)^g\) divides \(p_A\). \(\blacksquare\)

2.2 Diagonalizability

\(A\) is diagonalizable (over \(\mathbb{F}\)) if \(V\) has a basis of eigenvectors of \(A\). Equivalently, \(A = S\Lambda S^{-1}\) with \(\Lambda\) diagonal. The columns of \(S\) are the eigenvectors.

Theorem 2.3. \(A\) is diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{F}\) iff \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{F}\) and \(g_\lambda = a_\lambda\) for every \(\lambda \in \sigma(A)\). In particular, if \(A\) has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues in \(\mathbb{F}\), it is diagonalizable.

Proof. (⇒) If \(A = S\Lambda S^{-1}\), then \(p_A(t) = \prod_i (t - \Lambda_{ii})\) splits, and \(g_\lambda = \#\{i : \Lambda_{ii} = \lambda\} = a_\lambda\).

(⇐) By Lemma 2.1, the sum \(\sum_\lambda E_\lambda\) is direct. Its dimension is \(\sum_\lambda g_\lambda = \sum_\lambda a_\lambda = \deg p_A = n\). So it is all of \(V\), and the union of bases of the \(E_\lambda\) is an eigenbasis. \(\blacksquare\)

Examples from graphs. Let \(\vec P_n\) be the directed path \(1 \to 2 \to \dots \to n\) and \(\vec C_n\) the directed cycle.

  • The adjacency matrix of \(\vec P_n\) is nilpotent. Its only eigenvalue is \(0\), with \(a_0 = n\) but \(g_0 = 1\), so it is not diagonalizable over any field.
  • The adjacency matrix of \(\vec C_n\) is a permutation matrix with \(p(t) = t^n - 1\). It has \(n\) distinct complex eigenvalues (the \(n\)-th roots of unity), so it is diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{C}\). For \(n \ge 3\) it is not diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{R}\).
  • Undirected graphs give symmetric matrices, which are always diagonalizable, in an orthonormal basis (Section 6).

Why diagonalizability matters. If \(A = S\Lambda S^{-1}\), then \[ A^k = S\Lambda^k S^{-1}, \qquad e^{tA} = S e^{t\Lambda}S^{-1}, \] so linear dynamics \(x_{k+1} = Ax_k\) or \(\dot x = Ax\) decouple into \(n\) scalar ones. The catch for non-symmetric \(A\) is that \(S\) need not be orthogonal. An ill-conditioned \(S\) (large \(\kappa(S) = \|S\|\,\|S^{-1}\|\)) makes these formulas numerically and quantitatively misleading. See Section 5.6.

3. Polynomials in \(A\) and the minimal polynomial

3.1 The minimal polynomial

For \(q(t) = \sum_k c_k t^k \in \mathbb{F}[t]\), set \(q(A) = \sum_k c_k A^k\). The map \(q \mapsto q(A)\) respects sums and products. So all polynomials in \(A\) commute with each other and with \(A\). Two further facts:

  • If \(Av = \lambda v\), then \(q(A)v = q(\lambda)v\).
  • \(q(S^{-1}AS) = S^{-1}q(A)S\).

Some nonzero polynomial annihilates \(A\): the \(n^2 + 1\) matrices \(I, A, \dots, A^{n^2}\) live in the \(n^2\)-dimensional space \(\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\), so they are linearly dependent.

Proposition 3.1. There is a unique monic polynomial \(m_A\) of least degree with \(m_A(A) = 0\), the minimal polynomial of \(A\). A polynomial \(q\) satisfies \(q(A) = 0\) iff \(m_A\) divides \(q\).

Proof. Let \(m\) be a monic annihilating polynomial of least degree. For any \(q\) with \(q(A) = 0\), divide: \(q = sm + r\) with \(\deg r < \deg m\). Then \(r(A) = q(A) - s(A)m(A) = 0\), so \(r = 0\) by minimality. Uniqueness: two such \(m\) divide each other and are monic. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.2. The roots of \(m_A\) in \(\mathbb{F}\) are exactly the eigenvalues of \(A\) in \(\mathbb{F}\).

Proof. If \(Av = \lambda v\) with \(v \ne 0\), then \(0 = m_A(A)v = m_A(\lambda)v\), so \(m_A(\lambda) = 0\).

Conversely, if \(m_A(\lambda) = 0\), write \(m_A(t) = (t - \lambda)q(t)\). Since \(\deg q < \deg m_A\), \(q(A) \ne 0\). Pick \(w\) with \(v = q(A)w \ne 0\). Then \((A - \lambda I)v = m_A(A)w = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3.2 The primary decomposition

The engine behind everything that follows is a statement about coprime polynomials.

Lemma 3.3 (Kernel splitting). Suppose \(q(A) = 0\) and \(q = q_1q_2\) with \(\gcd(q_1, q_2) = 1\). Then \[ V = \ker q_1(A) \oplus \ker q_2(A). \] Both summands are \(A\)-invariant. The projection onto \(\ker q_1(A)\) along \(\ker q_2(A)\) is a polynomial in \(A\).

Proof. By Bézout, there are polynomials \(a, b\) with \(aq_1 + bq_2 = 1\), so \(I = a(A)q_1(A) + b(A)q_2(A)\). For any \(v\), \[ v = \underbrace{b(A)q_2(A)v}_{=:v_1} + \underbrace{a(A)q_1(A)v}_{=:v_2}. \] Here \(q_1(A)v_1 = b(A)q(A)v = 0\), so \(v_1 \in \ker q_1(A)\). Similarly, \(v_2 \in \ker q_2(A)\).

If \(v\) lies in both kernels, both terms vanish, so \(v = 0\). The sum is therefore direct, and the projection onto the first summand is \(b(A)q_2(A)\). Invariance: if \(q_i(A)v = 0\), then \(q_i(A)Av = Aq_i(A)v = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 3.4 (Primary decomposition). If \(q(A) = 0\) and \(q = q_1 \cdots q_r\) with the \(q_i\) pairwise coprime, then \[ V = \ker q_1(A) \oplus \dots \oplus \ker q_r(A). \] Each summand is \(A\)-invariant, and the projections \(P_i\) onto the summands are polynomials in \(A\). In particular, they commute with \(A\), with each other, and with anything that commutes with \(A\).

Proof. Induct on \(r\), applying Lemma 3.3 to \(q_1\) and \(q_2\cdots q_r\) (which are coprime). \(\blacksquare\)

3.3 Diagonalizability via the minimal polynomial

Theorem 3.5. The following are equivalent:

  1. \(A\) is diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{F}\).
  2. \(m_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{F}\) with distinct roots: \(m_A(t) = \prod_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)}(t - \lambda)\).
  3. \(A\) is annihilated by some polynomial that splits over \(\mathbb{F}\) with distinct roots.

Proof.

  • (1)⇒(2): Let \(q(t) = \prod_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)} (t-\lambda)\). Then \(q(A)\) kills every eigenvector, hence every vector of an eigenbasis. So \(q(A) = 0\) and \(m_A\) divides \(q\). By Proposition 3.2, every factor of \(q\) appears in \(m_A\), so \(m_A = q\).
  • (2)⇒(3) is trivial.
  • (3)⇒(1): If \(q(A) = 0\) with \(q = \prod_{i}(t - \mu_i)\) and the \(\mu_i\) distinct, the factors are pairwise coprime. Corollary 3.4 gives \(V = \bigoplus_i \ker(A - \mu_i I)\), a direct sum of eigenspaces. \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 3.6.

  1. If \(A\) is diagonalizable and \(W\) is an \(A\)-invariant subspace, then \(A|_W\) is diagonalizable. (Proof: \(m_A(A|_W) = 0\), so criterion (3) applies.)
  2. Projections (\(A^2 = A\)) and involutions (\(A^2 = I\)) are diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{R}\). Matrices with \(A^k = I\) are diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{C}\), since \(t^k - 1\) has \(k\) distinct complex roots. This covers every permutation matrix, and in particular \(\vec C_n\).

Proposition 3.7 (Simultaneous diagonalization). If \(A\) and \(B\) are diagonalizable and \(AB = BA\), then they have a common eigenbasis.

Proof. \(B\) preserves each eigenspace \(E_\lambda\) of \(A\): if \(Av = \lambda v\), then \(A(Bv) = BAv = \lambda Bv\). By Corollary 3.6(1), \(B|_{E_\lambda}\) is diagonalizable. Choose an eigenbasis of \(B|_{E_\lambda}\) inside each \(E_\lambda\). Its vectors are eigenvectors of \(A\) too, and together they form a basis of \(V = \bigoplus_\lambda E_\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)

3.4 The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

Theorem 3.8 (Cayley–Hamilton). \(p_A(A) = 0\). Consequently, \(m_A\) divides \(p_A\), and \(\deg m_A \le n\).

Proof. Let \(\operatorname{adj}\) denote the adjugate (transposed cofactor matrix), so that \(\operatorname{adj}(M)\,M = \det(M)\,I\). Apply this to \(M = tI - A\): \[ \operatorname{adj}(tI - A)\,(tI - A) = p_A(t)\, I . \] The entries of \(\operatorname{adj}(tI - A)\) are polynomials of degree \(\le n-1\) in \(t\). Write \(\operatorname{adj}(tI - A) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_k t^k\) with constant matrices \(B_k\), and \(p_A(t) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_k t^k\). Set \(B_{-1} = B_n = 0\) and compare coefficients of \(t^k\): \[ B_{k-1} - B_kA = c_k I, \qquad k = 0, \dots, n. \] Multiply the \(k\)-th identity on the right by \(A^k\) and sum over \(k\). The left side telescopes: \[ p_A(A) = \sum_{k=0}^n \big(B_{k-1}A^k - B_kA^{k+1}\big) = B_{-1} - B_nA^{n+1} = 0. \qquad\blacksquare \]

The proof uses only polynomial identities, so it holds over any field. The tempting one-liner “substitute \(t = A\) into \(\det(tI - A)\)” is not a proof, since \(\det(A\cdot I - A)\) would be a scalar, whereas \(p_A(A)\) is a matrix.

Corollary 3.9. If \(p_A(t) = \prod_\lambda (t - \lambda)^{a_\lambda}\), then \[ m_A(t) = \prod_\lambda (t-\lambda)^{k_\lambda}, \qquad 1 \le k_\lambda \le a_\lambda. \] The exponents \(k_\lambda\) are identified in Section 5 as the size of the largest Jordan block for \(\lambda\).

Examples. For \(A = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,2)\): \(p_A = (t-1)^2(t-2)\) and \(m_A = (t-1)(t-2)\). For the \(n\times n\) Jordan block with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) (Section 5): \(p_A = m_A = (t-\lambda)^n\).

Application: distinct eigenvalues bound the diameter. Let \(G\) be a connected graph with nonnegative edge weights, adjacency matrix \(A\), and diameter \(\mathrm{diam}(G)\). Then \[ \mathrm{diam}(G) \le (\text{number of distinct eigenvalues of } A) - 1. \]

Proof.

  • Since \(A\) is symmetric, it is diagonalizable (Section 6). By Theorem 3.5, \(\deg m_A\) equals the number of distinct eigenvalues.
  • Let \(D = \mathrm{diam}(G)\). Fix \(u\) and vertices \(w_0 = u, w_1, \dots, w_D\) along a shortest path, so \(d(u, w_k) = k\).
  • The entry \((A^j)_{u w_k}\) sums the weights of walks of length \(j\) from \(u\) to \(w_k\). So it is \(0\) for \(j < k\) and positive for \(j = k\).
  • If \(\sum_{j=0}^{D} c_j A^j = 0\), look at entry \((u, w_D)\): only \(j = D\) contributes, so \(c_D = 0\). Then look at \((u, w_{D-1})\) to get \(c_{D-1} = 0\), and so on.
  • So \(I, A, \dots, A^D\) are linearly independent, and \(\deg m_A \ge D + 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

The same fact underlies Krylov methods. Conjugate gradient and Lanczos applied to a symmetric matrix terminate, in exact arithmetic, after at most (number of distinct eigenvalues) steps.

4. Triangularization: the Schur decomposition and normal matrices

Not every matrix is diagonalizable, but over \(\mathbb{C}\) every matrix is triangularizable, even by a unitary change of basis. In this section \(V\) carries an inner product (Section 1).

Theorem 4.1 (Schur). Let \(A \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\). There is a unitary \(U\) and an upper triangular \(T\) with \[ A = UTU^*. \] The diagonal entries of \(T\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\), repeated according to algebraic multiplicity, and they can be placed in any prescribed order. If \(A\) is real and \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{R}\), then \(U\) can be taken real orthogonal and \(T\) real.

Proof. Induct on \(n\); the case \(n = 1\) is trivial. Let \(\lambda\) be the eigenvalue to be placed first. (One exists by the fundamental theorem of algebra, or by the splitting hypothesis in the real case.) Let \(v\) be a unit eigenvector, and complete it to a unitary matrix \([v\ W]\). Since \(W^*Av = \lambda W^*v = 0\), \[ [v\ W]^* A\, [v\ W] = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & v^*AW \\ 0 & W^*AW \end{pmatrix}. \] Now \(p_{W^*AW}(t) = p_A(t)/(t - \lambda)\). So by induction, \(W^*AW = U_1T_1U_1^*\), with the remaining eigenvalues in the prescribed order. Then \(U = [v\ \ WU_1]\) works. The diagonal of \(T\) lists the eigenvalues with multiplicity because \(p_A = p_T = \prod_i (t - T_{ii})\). \(\blacksquare\)

Unlike the Jordan form below, the Schur form is computed stably in practice: it is what the QR algorithm produces.

Theorem 4.2 (Normal matrices). \(A \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\) is unitarily diagonalizable (\(A = U\Lambda U^*\) with \(U\) unitary and \(\Lambda\) diagonal) iff \(A\) is normal: \(AA^* = A^*A\).

Proof. (⇒) Diagonal matrices commute with their adjoints, so \(AA^* = U\Lambda\bar\Lambda U^* = A^*A\).

(⇐) Write \(A = UTU^*\) by Schur. Then \(T\) is normal and upper triangular. Compare the \((1,1)\) entries of \(T^*T\) and \(TT^*\): \[ |t_{11}|^2 = (T^*T)_{11} = (TT^*)_{11} = \sum_{j} |t_{1j}|^2 . \] So \(t_{1j} = 0\) for \(j > 1\). The lower-right \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) block is again normal and upper triangular, so induction shows \(T\) is diagonal. \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 4.3. A Hermitian matrix is unitarily diagonalizable with real eigenvalues. (Here \(T = T^*\) is upper triangular, so it is diagonal with real entries.) A real symmetric matrix therefore has real eigenvalues, so \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{R}\), and the real case of Theorem 4.1 gives a real orthogonal eigenbasis.

This is already the finite-dimensional spectral theorem. Section 6 proves it again, variationally. That proof avoids the fundamental theorem of algebra, produces the Courant–Fischer characterization along the way, and is the one that extends to infinite dimensions (Part II).

Examples of normal matrices.

  • Hermitian and skew-Hermitian matrices.
  • Unitary matrices, including permutation matrices. The adjacency matrix of \(\vec C_n\) is unitarily diagonalized by the discrete Fourier basis \(\big(\omega^{jk}/\sqrt n\big)_{j}\) with \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}\).
  • Circulant matrices, i.e., polynomials in the cyclic shift. These are the adjacency matrices of Cayley graphs of \(\mathbb{Z}_n\), and they are all diagonalized by the same Fourier basis.

The adjacency matrix of a directed graph is normal only in special cases. The directed path is not even diagonalizable.

5. The Jordan canonical form

When \(A\) is not diagonalizable, the best one can do by similarity is block diagonal form with Jordan blocks. Throughout this section, assume \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{F}\), which is automatic over \(\mathbb{C}\). Write \(p_A(t) = \prod_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}(t-\lambda)^{a_\lambda}\).

5.1 Generalized eigenspaces

The generalized eigenspace of \(\lambda \in \sigma(A)\) is \[ G_\lambda = \ker (A - \lambda I)^{a_\lambda} \;\supseteq\; E_\lambda . \]

Theorem 5.1 (Generalized eigenspace decomposition).

  1. \(V = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} G_\lambda\), and each \(G_\lambda\) is \(A\)-invariant.
  2. \(\lambda\) is the only eigenvalue of \(A|_{G_\lambda}\), and \(\dim G_\lambda = a_\lambda\).
  3. The projections \(P_\lambda\) onto \(G_\lambda\) along \(\bigoplus_{\mu\ne\lambda} G_\mu\) are polynomials in \(A\), and \(\sum_\lambda P_\lambda = I\).

Proof. (1) and (3): By Cayley–Hamilton, \(p_A(A) = 0\). The factors \((t - \lambda)^{a_\lambda}\) are pairwise coprime, so Corollary 3.4 applies.

(2): If \(v \in G_\lambda\) and \(Av = \mu v\) with \(v \ne 0\), then \(0 = (A-\lambda I)^{a_\lambda}v = (\mu - \lambda)^{a_\lambda}v\), so \(\mu = \lambda\). Hence \(p_{A|_{G_\lambda}}(t) = (t - \lambda)^{\dim G_\lambda}\). In a basis adapted to the decomposition in (1), \(A\) is block diagonal, so \(p_A = \prod_\lambda p_{A|_{G_\lambda}}\). Comparing exponents gives \(\dim G_\lambda = a_\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)

5.2 The Jordan–Chevalley decomposition

Theorem 5.2 (Jordan–Chevalley). There are unique linear maps \(S\) and \(N\) with:

  • \(A = S + N\);
  • \(S\) diagonalizable and \(N\) nilpotent (\(N^k = 0\) for some \(k\));
  • \(SN = NS\).

Moreover, \(S\) and \(N\) are polynomials in \(A\).

Proof. Existence. With \(P_\lambda\) as in Theorem 5.1, set \(S = \sum_\lambda \lambda P_\lambda\) and \(N = A - S\). Both are polynomials in \(A\), so they commute. \(S\) acts as \(\lambda I\) on \(G_\lambda\), so it is diagonalizable. On \(G_\lambda\), \(N\) acts as \(A - \lambda I\), and \((A - \lambda I)^{a_\lambda} = 0\) there. So \(N^{\max_\lambda a_\lambda} = 0\).

Uniqueness. Suppose \(A = S' + N'\) is another such decomposition.

  • \(S'\) and \(N'\) commute with \(S' + N' = A\), hence with every polynomial in \(A\), in particular with \(S\) and \(N\).
  • So \(S - S' = N' - N\). The left side is a difference of commuting diagonalizable maps, hence diagonalizable by Proposition 3.7. The right side is a difference of commuting nilpotent maps, hence nilpotent: expand \((N' - N)^{2k}\) with the binomial theorem.
  • A map that is both diagonalizable and nilpotent is \(0\). \(\blacksquare\)

\(S = \sum_\lambda \lambda P_\lambda\) is the non-normal analogue of the spectral decomposition in Theorem 6.2. The differences are that the \(P_\lambda\) are oblique projections, not orthogonal ones, and that there is a nilpotent correction \(N\). When \(A\) is normal, \(N = 0\) and the \(P_\lambda\) are orthogonal.

5.3 Nilpotent maps

For \(\lambda \in \mathbb{F}\) and \(s \ge 1\), the Jordan block \(J_s(\lambda) \in \mathbb{F}^{s\times s}\) has \(\lambda\) on the diagonal, \(1\) on the superdiagonal, and \(0\) elsewhere: \[ J_s(\lambda) = \lambda I_s + N_s, \qquad N_s e_1 = 0,\quad N_s e_j = e_{j-1}\ (j \ge 2). \] \(N_s\) is nilpotent, with \(N_s^{s-1} \ne 0 = N_s^s\). So \(\dim\ker N_s^k = \min(k, s)\).

Theorem 5.3 (Structure of nilpotent maps). Let \(N : V \to V\) be nilpotent. There are vectors \(u_1, \dots, u_r \in V\) and integers \(m_1, \dots, m_r \ge 0\) such that:

  • the vectors \(N^{m_i}u_i, \dots, Nu_i, u_i\), for \(i = 1, \dots, r\), form a basis of \(V\);
  • \(N^{m_i + 1}u_i = 0\) for each \(i\).

In this basis, listed chain by chain, \(N = \bigoplus_i J_{m_i+1}(0)\).

Proof. Induct on \(\dim V\). If \(N = 0\), any basis works with all \(m_i = 0\). Otherwise, \(N\) is not injective (it is nilpotent), so \(\operatorname{range} N \subsetneq V\). \(N\) maps \(\operatorname{range} N\) into itself nilpotently.

Step 1: apply induction to \(\operatorname{range} N\). We get \(v_1, \dots, v_s \in \operatorname{range} N\) and integers \(\ell_i \ge 0\) such that the vectors \(N^j v_i\) (\(0 \le j \le \ell_i\)) form a basis of \(\operatorname{range} N\), and \(N^{\ell_i+1}v_i = 0\). Write \(v_i = Nu_i\).

Step 2: the lifted chains are independent. Let \(L\) be the list of vectors \(N^j u_i\) for \(0 \le j \le \ell_i + 1\). Suppose \(\sum_{i,j} c_{ij} N^j u_i = 0\). Apply \(N\): \[ \sum_{i,j} c_{ij}N^{j+1}u_i = \sum_i \sum_{j=0}^{\ell_i} c_{ij} N^j v_i = 0, \] where the terms with \(j = \ell_i + 1\) vanish because \(N^{\ell_i+1}v_i = 0\). The remaining vectors are the basis of \(\operatorname{range} N\), so \(c_{ij} = 0\) for \(j \le \ell_i\). What is left is \(\sum_i c_{i,\ell_i+1} N^{\ell_i+1}u_i = \sum_i c_{i,\ell_i+1}N^{\ell_i}v_i = 0\). These vectors are also part of that basis, so the remaining coefficients vanish too.

Step 3: complete with kernel vectors. Extend \(L\) to a basis of \(V\) by vectors \(w_1, \dots, w_p\). Each \(Nw_k\) lies in \(\operatorname{range} N\), which is spanned by \(N(L)\). So \(Nw_k = Nx_k\) for some \(x_k \in \operatorname{span} L\). Put \(u_{s+k} = w_k - x_k\). Then \(Nu_{s+k} = 0\), and \(L, u_{s+1}, \dots, u_{s+p}\) still spans \(V\) and has \(\dim V\) elements, so it is a basis.

The theorem holds with \(m_i = \ell_i + 1\) for \(i \le s\) and \(m_{s+k} = 0\). For the matrix form, in the chain \(N^{m}u, \dots, Nu, u\), \(N\) sends each vector to the previous one and the first to \(0\). That is exactly \(N_{m+1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

5.4 The Jordan canonical form

Theorem 5.4 (Jordan canonical form). If \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb{F}\), there is a basis of \(V\) in which \[ A = \bigoplus_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\ \bigoplus_{i=1}^{g_\lambda} J_{s_{\lambda,i}}(\lambda). \] The block sizes are determined by \(A\): for each \(\lambda\) and \(k \ge 1\), \[ \#\{i : s_{\lambda,i} \ge k\} = \dim\ker(A - \lambda I)^k - \dim\ker(A-\lambda I)^{k-1}. \] Hence the Jordan form is unique up to the order of the blocks. Two matrices are similar iff they have the same Jordan form.

Proof. Existence. By Theorem 5.1, it suffices to work on each \(G_\lambda\). There, \(N = (A - \lambda I)|_{G_\lambda}\) is nilpotent. Theorem 5.3 gives a basis of \(G_\lambda\) in which \(N = \bigoplus_i J_{s_i}(0)\), so \(A|_{G_\lambda} = \lambda I + N = \bigoplus_i J_{s_i}(\lambda)\).

The counting formula. \((A - \lambda I)^k\) is invertible on \(G_\mu\) for \(\mu \ne \lambda\). So only the \(\lambda\)-blocks contribute to its kernel, and \(\dim\ker J_s(\lambda - \lambda)^k = \dim \ker N_s^k = \min(k,s)\). Hence \[ \dim\ker(A-\lambda I)^k = \sum_i \min(k, s_{\lambda,i}). \] Taking differences in \(k\) counts the blocks of size \(\ge k\).

The number of blocks for \(\lambda\) is the \(k=1\) count, \(\dim E_\lambda = g_\lambda\).

Similarity. The dimensions of \(\ker(A - \lambda I)^k\) are similarity invariants, so similar matrices have the same Jordan form. The converse is clear. \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 5.5 (Reading the invariants off the Jordan form). For each \(\lambda \in \sigma(A)\):

  • \(a_\lambda\) is the total size of the \(\lambda\)-blocks;
  • \(g_\lambda\) is the number of \(\lambda\)-blocks;
  • the exponent \(k_\lambda\) of \((t-\lambda)\) in \(m_A\) is the size of the largest \(\lambda\)-block.

(The last follows because \(m_A\) of a block diagonal matrix is the least common multiple of the blocks’ minimal polynomials, and \(m_{J_s(\lambda)} = (t-\lambda)^s\).) In particular, \(A\) is diagonalizable iff every Jordan block is \(1\times1\), iff \(k_\lambda = 1\) for all \(\lambda\), iff \(g_\lambda = a_\lambda\) for all \(\lambda\). This recovers Theorems 2.3 and 3.5.

Example. \(\vec P_n\) is a single block \(J_n(0)\) (Exercise 5). A matrix with \(p_A = (t-2)^4\), \(\dim\ker(A - 2I) = 2\) and \(\dim\ker(A-2I)^2 = 4\) has Jordan form \(J_2(2)\oplus J_2(2)\).

5.5 Powers and exponentials

Since \(\lambda I\) and \(N_s\) commute and \(N_s^s = 0\), the binomial theorem gives \[ J_s(\lambda)^k = \sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\binom{k}{j}\lambda^{k-j}N_s^j, \qquad e^{tJ_s(\lambda)} = e^{t\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\frac{t^j}{j!}N_s^j . \] So the entries of \(A^k\) are combinations of \(k^j\lambda^k\) with \(j <\) (block size). Consequences:

  • \(A^k \to 0\) iff the spectral radius \(\rho(A) = \max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\lambda|\) is less than \(1\). (A block \(J_s(\lambda)\) with \(|\lambda| < 1\) still decays, because \(k^{s-1}|\lambda|^k \to 0\).)
  • More precisely, if \(\rho(A) > 0\), then \(\|A^k\| \asymp k^{s-1}\rho(A)^k\) as \(k\to\infty\), where \(s\) is the largest block among the eigenvalues of modulus \(\rho(A)\). The limit \(\|A^k\|^{1/k} \to \rho(A)\) is Gelfand’s formula.
  • For a Markov chain \(P\), convergence to stationarity is governed by the eigenvalues with \(|\lambda| < 1\). For a non-reversible chain, Jordan blocks contribute polynomial prefactors, and \(\kappa(S)\) can produce long transients before the asymptotic rate kicks in. Reversible chains avoid both problems because \(P\) is self-adjoint in \(\ell^2(\pi)\) (Section 6.5).

5.6 A warning: the Jordan form is unstable

The Jordan form is a discontinuous function of the matrix. Let \(A_\varepsilon = J_n(0) + \varepsilon\, e_ne_1^\top\). Then \(p_{A_\varepsilon}(t) = t^n - \varepsilon\). So \(A_\varepsilon\) has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues \(\varepsilon^{1/n}e^{2\pi ij/n}\) and is diagonalizable. For \(n = 10\) and \(\varepsilon = 10^{-10}\), a perturbation of size \(10^{-10}\) moves the eigenvalues by \(0.1\).

Compare the Hermitian case, where Weyl’s inequality (Corollary 6.9) says eigenvalues move by at most \(\|E\|\). For diagonalizable matrices, the general statement interpolates between the two:

Theorem 5.6 (Bauer–Fike). If \(A = S\Lambda S^{-1}\) is diagonalizable, then every eigenvalue \(\mu\) of \(A + E\) satisfies \[ \min_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\mu - \lambda| \le \kappa(S)\,\|E\|, \qquad \kappa(S) = \|S\|\,\|S^{-1}\|. \]

Proof. Assume \(\mu \notin \sigma(A)\) and let \((A + E)v = \mu v\) with \(v \ne 0\). Then \[ v = -(A - \mu I)^{-1}Ev = -S(\Lambda - \mu I)^{-1}S^{-1}Ev. \] Taking norms, \(1 \le \kappa(S)\,\|E\| \,/\min_\lambda|\lambda - \mu|\). \(\blacksquare\)

For normal matrices \(S\) is unitary, so \(\kappa(S) = 1\). In practice, one therefore works with the Schur form, singular values, or pseudospectra (Trefethen & Embree) rather than the Jordan form. The Jordan form remains the right theoretical description of what \(A\) does.

6. The spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators

Throughout this section, \(V\) is a finite-dimensional inner product space over \(\mathbb{F}\) with \(n = \dim V \ge 1\), and \(A : V \to V\) is linear.

Corollary 4.3 already gives the spectral theorem as a consequence of the Schur decomposition. Here we prove it again from scratch, variationally. This proof does not use the fundamental theorem of algebra, it produces the Rayleigh-quotient characterization of eigenvalues on which the rest of the course depends, and it is the proof that generalizes to compact operators in Part II.

6.1 Two elementary facts

Lemma 6.1. Let \(A\) be self-adjoint.

  1. \(\langle Ax, x\rangle \in \mathbb{R}\) for every \(x\).
  2. Every eigenvalue of \(A\) is real.
  3. Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  4. If \(W\) is a subspace with \(A W \subseteq W\), then \(A W^\perp \subseteq W^\perp\).

Proof. (1) \(\langle Ax, x\rangle = \langle x, Ax\rangle = \overline{\langle Ax, x\rangle}\). (2) If \(Ax = \lambda x\) with \(x \ne 0\), then \(\lambda \|x\|^2 = \langle Ax, x\rangle \in \mathbb{R}\). (3) If \(Ax = \lambda x\) and \(Ay = \mu y\) with \(\lambda \ne \mu\) (both real by (2)), then \(\lambda\langle x, y\rangle = \langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, Ay\rangle = \mu \langle x, y\rangle\), so \(\langle x, y\rangle = 0\). (4) If \(y \in W^\perp\) and \(w \in W\), then \(\langle Ay, w\rangle = \langle y, Aw\rangle = 0\) because \(Aw \in W\). \(\blacksquare\)

6.2 Statement

Theorem 6.2 (Spectral theorem, finite dimensions). Let \(A : V \to V\) be self-adjoint. Then there is an orthonormal basis \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) of \(V\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(A\), with real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n\). Equivalently:

  • (Matrix form) If \(A \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\) is Hermitian (real symmetric when \(\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{R}\)), then \(A = Q \Lambda Q^*\), where \(Q\) is unitary (real orthogonal when \(\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}\)) with columns \(v_i\) and \(\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)\).
  • (Spectral decomposition) \(A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\, v_i v_i^*\). Grouping equal eigenvalues, \(A = \sum_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} \lambda P_\lambda\), where \(P_\lambda\) is the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace \(\ker(A - \lambda I)\). These projections are mutually orthogonal and sum to \(I\).

Note that the real case does not follow from the complex case by a formality: we need the eigenvectors of a real symmetric matrix to be real. The proof below works verbatim over either field.

6.3 Proof

The proof is variational. It never mentions the characteristic polynomial and so does not need the fundamental theorem of algebra. This is the version that generalizes to infinite dimensions.

Lemma 6.3 (Top eigenvector via the Rayleigh quotient). Let \(A\) be self-adjoint on \(V \ne \{0\}\). The function \(x \mapsto \langle Ax, x\rangle\) attains its maximum over the unit sphere \(S = \{x : \|x\| = 1\}\). Any maximizer \(x_\star\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_\star = \langle Ax_\star, x_\star\rangle = \max_{x \ne 0} R_A(x)\).

Proof. Choosing an orthonormal basis identifies \(V\) isometrically with \(\mathbb{F}^n\). The unit sphere is therefore closed and bounded, hence compact. The map \(x \mapsto \langle Ax, x\rangle\) is continuous and real-valued (Lemma 6.1), so it attains a maximum \(\lambda_\star\) at some \(x_\star \in S\).

Fix any \(y \perp x_\star\) and a scalar \(s \in \mathbb{F}\). For real \(t\), set \(u(t) = x_\star + t s y\). Then \(\|u(t)\|^2 = 1 + t^2 |s|^2 \|y\|^2\), and by maximality \(\langle A u(t), u(t)\rangle \le \lambda_\star \|u(t)\|^2\). Expanding both sides: \[ \lambda_\star + 2t\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\bar s\,\langle A x_\star, y\rangle\big) + t^2 |s|^2 \langle Ay, y\rangle \;\le\; \lambda_\star + t^2 \lambda_\star |s|^2 \|y\|^2 . \] Here we used \(\langle Ay, x_\star\rangle = \overline{\langle Ax_\star, y\rangle}\). Cancel \(\lambda_\star\), divide by \(t\), and let \(t \to 0^+\) and \(t \to 0^-\). This gives \(\mathrm{Re}(\bar s \langle Ax_\star, y\rangle) = 0\). Taking \(s = 1\) (and also \(s = i\) in the complex case) yields \(\langle Ax_\star, y\rangle = 0\).

So \(Ax_\star \perp \{x_\star\}^\perp\), which means \(Ax_\star \in \operatorname{span}\{x_\star\}\). Write \(Ax_\star = \mu x_\star\). Then \(\mu = \langle Ax_\star, x_\star\rangle = \lambda_\star\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proof of Theorem 6.2. Induct on \(n = \dim V\). If \(n = 1\), any unit vector works. For \(n > 1\), Lemma 6.3 gives a unit eigenvector \(v_1\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 = \max R_A\). The line \(W = \operatorname{span}\{v_1\}\) is \(A\)-invariant, so by Lemma 6.1(4), \(W^\perp\) is \(A\)-invariant too. The restriction \(A|_{W^\perp}\) is self-adjoint on the \((n-1)\)-dimensional inner product space \(W^\perp\). By induction it has an orthonormal eigenbasis \(v_2, \dots, v_n\) of \(W^\perp\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n\). Each \(\lambda_j\) with \(j \ge 2\) is a value of \(R_A\), so \(\lambda_j \le \lambda_1\). Then \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) is the required basis.

For the matrix form, let \(Q = [v_1 \cdots v_n]\). Then \(Q^*Q = I\) and \(AQ = Q\Lambda\). For the spectral decomposition, expand \(x = \sum_i \langle x, v_i\rangle v_i\) and apply \(A\): \(Ax = \sum_i \lambda_i \langle x, v_i\rangle v_i = \big(\sum_i \lambda_i v_i v_i^*\big) x\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 6.4. The same proof shows that \(\lambda_1 = \max_{\|x\|=1} \langle Ax, x\rangle\) and \(\lambda_n = \min_{\|x\|=1} \langle Ax, x\rangle\). Part II (Section 7) replaces the compactness of the unit sphere, which fails in infinite dimensions, with the compactness of the operator.

Remark 6.5 (Beyond self-adjoint). By Theorem 4.2, a complex matrix is unitarily diagonalizable iff it is normal. Non-normal matrices may fail to be diagonalizable at all (Section 5). Even when they are diagonalizable, their eigenvalues can be highly sensitive to perturbation (Section 5.6). Directed graphs and non-reversible Markov chains produce such matrices, and there pseudospectra are often the better tool (Trefethen & Embree).

6.4 Consequences

Throughout, \(A\) is self-adjoint with eigenpairs \((\lambda_i, v_i)\) as in Theorem 6.2, \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n\).

Corollary 6.6 (Functional calculus). For any function \(f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{F}\), define \(f(A) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) v_i v_i^*\). This agrees with the obvious definition when \(f\) is a polynomial, so, for example, \(A^k = \sum_i \lambda_i^k v_i v_i^*\). Standard instances:

  • the heat kernel \(e^{-tL}\);
  • the pseudoinverse \(L^{+} = \sum_{\lambda_i \ne 0} \lambda_i^{-1} v_i v_i^*\);
  • the square root \(A^{1/2}\) for \(A \succeq 0\);
  • spectral projections \(\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(A)\).

Corollary 6.7 (Quantities read off the spectrum).

  • \(\operatorname{tr} A = \sum_i \lambda_i\) and \(\det A = \prod_i \lambda_i\).
  • \(\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}} = \max_i |\lambda_i|\) and \(\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_i \lambda_i^2\).
  • \(A \succeq 0\) iff all \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) iff \(A = B^*B\) for some \(B\).
  • \(\operatorname{rank} A\) is the number of nonzero \(\lambda_i\).
  • \(\ker A\) and \(\operatorname{range} A\) are orthogonal complements, spanned by the eigenvectors with zero and nonzero eigenvalues respectively.

Theorem 6.8 (Courant–Fischer min–max). For \(k = 1, \dots, n\), \[ \lambda_k \;=\; \max_{\substack{S \subseteq V\\ \dim S = k}} \; \min_{x \in S\setminus\{0\}} R_A(x) \;=\; \min_{\substack{T \subseteq V\\ \dim T = n-k+1}} \; \max_{x \in T\setminus\{0\}} R_A(x). \]

Proof. We prove the max–min formula; the min–max formula follows by applying it to \(-A\).

In eigenvector coordinates, \(x = \sum_i c_i v_i\) and \(R_A(x) = \sum_i \lambda_i |c_i|^2 / \sum_i |c_i|^2\), a weighted average of the eigenvalues.

(\(\ge\)) Take \(S = \operatorname{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\). Every \(x \in S\) is an average of \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k\), so \(R_A(x) \ge \lambda_k\).

(\(\le\)) Let \(S\) be any \(k\)-dimensional subspace and \(U = \operatorname{span}\{v_k, \dots, v_n\}\), which has dimension \(n - k + 1\). Since \(\dim S + \dim U = n+1 > n\), there is a nonzero \(x \in S \cap U\). For this \(x\), \(R_A(x)\) is an average of \(\lambda_k, \dots, \lambda_n\), so \(R_A(x) \le \lambda_k\). Hence \(\min_{S} R_A \le \lambda_k\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example. For a graph Laplacian \(L\) with eigenvalues \(0 = \mu_1 \le \mu_2 \le \dots \le \mu_n\), the min-version gives \(\mu_2 = \min_{x \perp \mathbf{1},\, x \ne 0} \dfrac{\sum_{(u,v)\in E} w_{uv}(x_u - x_v)^2}{\sum_u x_u^2}\). This is the starting point for Cheeger’s inequality and spectral partitioning.

Corollary 6.9 (Weyl’s perturbation inequality). If \(A, B\) are self-adjoint with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1(\cdot) \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n(\cdot)\), then for every \(k\), \[ |\lambda_k(A) - \lambda_k(B)| \le \|A - B\|_{\mathrm{op}}. \]

Proof. For every \(x \ne 0\), \(R_A(x) = R_B(x) + R_{A-B}(x) \le R_B(x) + \|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\). Take max–min over \(S\) in Theorem 6.8 to get \(\lambda_k(A) \le \lambda_k(B) + \|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\). Swap \(A\) and \(B\) for the other direction. \(\blacksquare\)

Weyl’s inequality controls eigenvalues. Eigenvectors are controlled only relative to the gap separating their eigenvalue from the rest of the spectrum (Davis–Kahan \(\sin\Theta\) theorem; see the separate supplement). We will return to this when analyzing spectral clustering.

6.5 Self-adjointness for a weighted inner product

Theorem 6.2 is stated for an abstract inner product space. Nothing in it refers to the standard inner product on \(\mathbb{F}^n\). This matters because several natural graph matrices are not symmetric, yet are self-adjoint for a different inner product.

Let \(M \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\) be positive definite (\(M = M^*\) and \(M \succ 0\)). Then \(\langle x, y\rangle_M = y^* M x\) is an inner product on \(\mathbb{F}^n\). The adjoint of \(B\) with respect to it is \(M^{-1}B^*M\), because \[ \langle Bx, y\rangle_M = y^* M B x = (M^{-1} B^* M y)^* M x = \langle x, M^{-1}B^*My\rangle_M . \]

Proposition 6.10. Let \(M \succ 0\) and \(B \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\). The following are equivalent:

  1. \(B\) is self-adjoint with respect to \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_M\);
  2. \(MB\) is self-adjoint (Hermitian) in the usual sense;
  3. \(M^{1/2} B M^{-1/2}\) is self-adjoint in the usual sense.

When they hold, \(B\) has real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n\), and there is a basis of eigenvectors that is orthonormal for \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_M\). Explicitly, if \(u_1, \dots, u_n\) is an orthonormal eigenbasis of \(M^{1/2}BM^{-1/2}\), then \(v_i = M^{-1/2}u_i\) satisfies \(Bv_i = \lambda_i v_i\) and \(v_i^* M v_j = \delta_{ij}\). Courant–Fischer (Theorem 6.8) holds with the Rayleigh quotient \[ R^M_B(x) = \frac{\langle Bx, x\rangle_M}{\langle x, x\rangle_M} = \frac{x^* MB\, x}{x^* M x}. \]

Proof.

  • (1)⇔(2): (1) says \(M^{-1}B^*M = B\), i.e., \(B^*M = MB\). Since \((MB)^* = B^*M\), this is exactly (2).
  • (2)⇔(3): \(M^{1/2}BM^{-1/2} = M^{-1/2}(MB)M^{-1/2}\). A congruence by the invertible Hermitian matrix \(M^{-1/2}\) preserves being Hermitian, and so does its inverse.
  • The spectral statements: apply Theorem 6.2 to \(C = M^{1/2}BM^{-1/2}\). From \(Cu_i = \lambda_i u_i\) we get \(B(M^{-1/2}u_i) = \lambda_i M^{-1/2}u_i\), and \(v_i^*Mv_j = u_i^* u_j = \delta_{ij}\). Alternatively, apply Theorem 6.2 directly to the inner product space \((\mathbb{F}^n, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_M)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Conceptually, \(U = M^{1/2}\) is an isometry from \((\mathbb{F}^n, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_M)\) onto \((\mathbb{F}^n, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)\), since \(\langle Ux, Uy\rangle = y^*Mx = \langle x, y\rangle_M\). The operator \(B\) in the first picture corresponds to \(UBU^{-1} = M^{1/2}BM^{-1/2}\) in the second. Isometries preserve adjoints, so \(B\) is self-adjoint in one picture iff it is in the other. Proposition 6.10 is this observation written in coordinates.

Application: the random-walk and normalized Laplacians. Let \(G\) be a weighted graph with no isolated vertices, so \(D \succ 0\). Define the degree inner product \(\langle x, y\rangle_D = \sum_u d_u\, x_u \bar y_u\), and the random-walk Laplacian \[ L_{\mathrm{rw}} = D^{-1}L = I - D^{-1}A. \] In the analysis-on-graphs convention, this is simply “the Laplacian”: \((L_{\mathrm{rw}} f)(u) = f(u) - \frac{1}{d_u}\sum_v w_{uv} f(v)\). Apply Proposition 6.10 with \(M = D\) and \(B = L_{\mathrm{rw}}\):

  • \(D L_{\mathrm{rw}} = L\), which is symmetric, so condition (2) holds. So \(L_{\mathrm{rw}}\) is self-adjoint for \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_D\).
  • \(D^{1/2} L_{\mathrm{rw}} D^{-1/2} = D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2} = \mathcal{L}\). So condition (3) is precisely the symmetry of the normalized Laplacian in the standard inner product.

Thus the statements “\(L_{\mathrm{rw}}\) is self-adjoint on \((\mathbb{R}^V, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_D)\)” and “\(\mathcal{L}\) is symmetric on \((\mathbb{R}^V, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)\)” are the same fact, seen through the isometry \(x \mapsto D^{1/2}x\). Consequences:

  • \(L_{\mathrm{rw}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}\) have the same (real) eigenvalues.

  • The eigenvectors are related by \(v = D^{-1/2}u\), and the eigenvectors of \(L_{\mathrm{rw}}\) are \(D\)-orthogonal.

  • Equivalently, the \(v_i\) solve the generalized eigenproblem \(Lv = \lambda Dv\).

  • The Rayleigh quotient becomes \[ R^D_{L_{\mathrm{rw}}}(x) = \frac{x^\top L x}{x^\top D x} = \frac{\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E} w_{uv}(x_u - x_v)^2}{\sum_u d_u x_u^2}. \] This is the form that appears in Cheeger’s inequality. The constant vector \(\mathbf 1\) spans the bottom eigenspace for a connected graph, and “orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\)” now means \(\langle x, \mathbf 1\rangle_D = \sum_u d_u x_u = 0\).

  • The same holds for \(P = D^{-1}A = I - L_{\mathrm{rw}}\). Since the stationary distribution of the random walk is \(\pi_u = d_u / \sum_v d_v\), \(P\) is self-adjoint on \(\ell^2(\pi)\). More generally, a Markov chain is reversible iff its transition matrix is self-adjoint on \(\ell^2(\pi)\).

Which picture to use is a matter of convenience. \(\mathcal{L}\) is convenient for computation and for applying matrix inequalities stated for symmetric matrices. \(L_{\mathrm{rw}}\) with \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_D\) is convenient for interpretation, because its eigenvectors are functions on the vertices, not reweighted ones. This is also the version that generalizes to infinite graphs and to manifolds, via \(\ell^2(V, d)\) and \(L^2(\mu)\).

  2. Prove the min–max half of Theorem 6.8 directly, without passing to \(-A\).
  3. Let \(A \succeq 0\). Show that \(A\) has a unique PSD square root. (Hint: any PSD square root commutes with \(A\), so it preserves each eigenspace of \(A\).)
  4. Using Section 6.5, prove that the random-walk matrix \(P = D^{-1}A\) of a connected graph has eigenvalues in \([-1,1]\), that \(1\) is a simple eigenvalue, and that \(-1\) is an eigenvalue iff the graph is bipartite.
  5. Compute the characteristic polynomial, minimal polynomial, and Jordan form of the adjacency matrices of the directed path \(\vec P_n\) and the directed cycle \(\vec C_n\). Find an eigenbasis of \(\vec C_n\) over \(\mathbb{C}\).
  6. Assume \(p_A\) splits. Show that \(A\) is diagonalizable iff \(\ker(A - \lambda I)^2 = \ker(A - \lambda I)\) for every \(\lambda \in \sigma(A)\).
  7. Show that every square matrix is similar to its transpose. (Hint: \(J_s(\lambda)^\top\) is similar to \(J_s(\lambda)\) via the reversal permutation.)
  8. Show that commuting matrices \(A, B \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\) have a common eigenvector, and deduce that they can be simultaneously upper-triangularized by a unitary matrix.
  9. Show that \(A^k \to 0\) iff \(\rho(A) < 1\), and give an example where \(\rho(A) < 1\) but \(\|A^k\|\) first grows to \(10^6\) before decaying. (Hint: \(2\times 2\) upper triangular.)
  10. Show that the number of distinct eigenvalues of the Laplacian of a connected graph is also at least \(\mathrm{diam}(G) + 1\). Compute both sides for the path \(P_n\) and the complete graph \(K_n\).
