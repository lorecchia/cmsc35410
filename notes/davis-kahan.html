The Davis–Kahan sin Θ Theorem

Supplement to the linear algebra review

Draft. For the spectral clustering lecture.

Weyl’s inequality (Review Part I, Corollary 6.9) says that eigenvalues of a self-adjoint matrix move by at most \(\|E\|\) under a perturbation \(E\). Eigenvectors are more fragile. If two eigenvalues are close, an arbitrarily small perturbation can rotate the corresponding eigenvectors by \(90°\). The Davis–Kahan theorem says this is the only obstruction: an invariant subspace is stable, with error controlled by \(\|E\|\) divided by the eigengap separating it from the rest of the spectrum.

Notation follows Part I of the review: \(\mathbb{F} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}\), \(M^*\) is the conjugate transpose, and \(\|\cdot\|\) is the operator (spectral) norm unless marked otherwise.

1. Motivating example

Let \(A = \operatorname{diag}(1+\varepsilon, 1)\) and \(\hat A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \varepsilon/2 & \varepsilon/2 \\ \varepsilon/2 & 1 + \varepsilon/2\end{pmatrix}\).

  • The top eigenvector of \(A\) is \(e_1\).
  • The top eigenvector of \(\hat A\) is \((e_1 + e_2)/\sqrt2\).
  • The perturbation \(\hat A - A = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) has norm \(\varepsilon/\sqrt2\), which is tiny.
  • Yet the top eigenvector rotated by \(45°\).

The perturbation is on the same scale as the eigengap \(\varepsilon\), so no eigenvector stability can be expected. By contrast, the two-dimensional eigenspace \(\operatorname{span}\{e_1, e_2\}\) did not move at all.

Lesson: bound the distance between subspaces, and measure the perturbation relative to the gap.

2. Distances between subspaces

Let \(V, \hat V \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times k}\) have orthonormal columns (\(V^*V = \hat V^*\hat V = I_k\)), spanning subspaces \(\mathcal V\) and \(\hat{\mathcal V}\).

Principal angles. The singular values of \(\hat V^* V \in \mathbb{F}^{k\times k}\) lie in \([0,1]\). Write them as \(\cos\theta_1 \ge \dots \ge \cos\theta_k\), with \(\theta_i \in [0, \pi/2]\). The \(\theta_i\) are the principal angles between \(\mathcal V\) and \(\hat{\mathcal V}\). They do not depend on the choice of orthonormal bases. Set \[ \sin\Theta(V, \hat V) = \operatorname{diag}(\sin\theta_1, \dots, \sin\theta_k). \]

Lemma 2.1. Let \(\hat V_\perp \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times(n-k)}\) complete \(\hat V\) to a unitary matrix \([\hat V\ \hat V_\perp]\).

  1. The singular values of \(\hat V_\perp^* V\) are \(\sin\theta_1, \dots, \sin\theta_k\), padded with zeros if \(n - k > k\).
  2. \(\|VV^* - \hat V \hat V^*\| = \|\sin\Theta\| = \sin\theta_1\), and \(\|VV^* - \hat V\hat V^*\|_F = \sqrt2\,\|\sin\Theta\|_F\).
  3. There is a unitary \(O \in \mathbb{F}^{k\times k}\) with \(\|\hat V O - V\|_F \le \sqrt2\,\|\sin\Theta\|_F\).

Proof. (1) Since \(\hat V\hat V^* + \hat V_\perp \hat V_\perp^* = I\), \[ I_k = V^*V = (\hat V^*V)^*(\hat V^*V) + (\hat V_\perp^* V)^*(\hat V_\perp^* V). \] So the eigenvalues of \((\hat V_\perp^*V)^*(\hat V_\perp^*V)\) are \(1 - \cos^2\theta_i = \sin^2\theta_i\).

  1. In the orthonormal basis \([\hat V\ \hat V_\perp]\), a direct computation from (1) shows that \(VV^* - \hat V\hat V^*\) has eigenvalues \(\pm\sin\theta_i\), plus zeros. Both identities follow. (Exercise: carry out the \(2\times 2\) block computation.)

  2. Take an SVD \(\hat V^*V = U (\cos\Theta) W^*\) and set \(O = UW^*\). Then \[ \|\hat VO - V\|_F^2 = 2k - 2\,\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{tr}(O^*\hat V^*V) = 2\sum_i (1 - \cos\theta_i) \le 2\sum_i \sin^2\theta_i, \] using \(1 - c \le 1 - c^2\) for \(c \in [0,1]\). \(\blacksquare\)

Part (3) is what spectral clustering needs. The embedding computed from data, the rows of \(\hat V\), matches the ideal embedding (the rows of \(V\)) up to a rotation \(O\). A rotation does not affect \(k\)-means, since it preserves all pairwise distances.

3. The theorem

Theorem 3.1 (Davis–Kahan \(\sin\Theta\) theorem). Let \(A\) and \(\hat A = A + E\) be self-adjoint \(n\times n\) matrices.

  • Let \(V \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times k}\) have orthonormal columns with \(AV = V\Lambda_1\), where \(\Lambda_1\) is diagonal with entries in an interval \([a, b]\).
  • Let \(\hat V_\perp \in \mathbb{F}^{n\times(n-k)}\) have orthonormal columns with \(\hat A \hat V_\perp = \hat V_\perp \hat\Lambda_2\), where \(\hat\Lambda_2\) is diagonal with every entry outside \((a - \delta, b + \delta)\), for some \(\delta > 0\).

Let \(\hat V\) be the orthonormal complement of \(\hat V_\perp\). It spans the remaining \(k\) eigenvectors of \(\hat A\). Then for the operator norm and for the Frobenius norm, \[ \|\sin\Theta(V, \hat V)\| \;\le\; \frac{\|EV\|}{\delta} \;\le\; \frac{\|E\|}{\delta}, \qquad \|\sin\Theta(V, \hat V)\|_F \;\le\; \frac{\|EV\|_F}{\delta}. \]

In words: if the unperturbed eigenvalues of interest sit in \([a,b]\), and the perturbed eigenvalues we discard stay at distance \(\delta\) from that interval, then the subspace moves by at most \(\|E\|/\delta\).

3.1 The Sylvester lemma

Lemma 3.2. Let \(\Lambda \in \mathbb{F}^{k\times k}\) and \(D \in \mathbb{F}^{m\times m}\) be self-adjoint. Suppose the eigenvalues of \(\Lambda\) lie in \([a,b]\) and those of \(D\) lie outside \((a - \delta, b+\delta)\). Then for every \(X \in \mathbb{F}^{m\times k}\) and \(\|\cdot\| \in \{\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}, \|\cdot\|_F\}\), \[ \|DX - X\Lambda\| \ge \delta\,\|X\|. \]

Proof. Let \(c = (a+b)/2\) and \(r = (b-a)/2\). Then \(\|\Lambda - cI\| \le r\). Every eigenvalue of \(D - cI\) has absolute value at least \(r + \delta\), so \(D - cI\) is invertible with \(\|(D - cI)^{-1}\| \le 1/(r+\delta)\).

Set \(Y = DX - X\Lambda = (D - cI)X - X(\Lambda - cI)\). Then \(X = (D-cI)^{-1}\big(Y + X(\Lambda - cI)\big)\). Using \(\|PQ\| \le \|P\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|Q\|\) and \(\|QP\| \le \|Q\|\,\|P\|_{\mathrm{op}}\), which hold for both norms, \[ \|X\| \le \frac{\|Y\| + r\|X\|}{r + \delta}. \] Rearranging gives \(\delta\|X\| \le \|Y\|\). \(\blacksquare\)

The centering trick is what makes the lemma work in operator norm. For the Frobenius norm alone there is a one-line proof when \(D\) and \(\Lambda\) are diagonal: \((DX - X\Lambda)_{ij} = (d_i - \lambda_j)X_{ij}\) and \(|d_i - \lambda_j| \ge \delta\).

3.2 Proof of Theorem 3.1

Let \(X = \hat V_\perp^* V\). By Lemma 2.1(1), \(\|X\| = \|\sin\Theta\|\) and \(\|X\|_F = \|\sin\Theta\|_F\). Multiply the identity \(\hat A V - V\Lambda_1 = (A + E)V - V\Lambda_1 = EV\) on the left by \(\hat V_\perp^*\), and use \(\hat V_\perp^*\hat A = \hat\Lambda_2\hat V_\perp^*\) (valid because \(\hat A\) is self-adjoint). This gives the Sylvester equation \[ \hat\Lambda_2 X - X\Lambda_1 = \hat V_\perp^* E V. \] By Lemma 3.2, \(\delta\|X\| \le \|\hat V_\perp^* EV\| \le \|EV\|\). \(\blacksquare\)

The whole proof is two lines once the Sylvester equation is written down. The eigengap enters only through Lemma 3.2.

3.3 A version with the gap measured on \(A\) alone

In applications, \(A\) is an idealized (population) matrix whose spectrum we know, and \(\hat A\) is observed. The natural gap is that of \(A\). For the top-\(k\) eigenspace, let \[ \delta_0 = \lambda_k(A) - \lambda_{k+1}(A) > 0, \] where \(\lambda_1(\cdot) \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n(\cdot)\).

Corollary 3.3. Let \(V\) and \(\hat V\) span the top-\(k\) eigenspaces of \(A\) and \(\hat A = A + E\). Then \[ \|\sin\Theta(V,\hat V)\| \le \frac{2\|E\|}{\delta_0}, \qquad \|\sin\Theta(V, \hat V)\|_F \le \frac{2\sqrt k\,\|E\|}{\delta_0}. \]

Proof. Suppose first \(\|E\| \le \delta_0/2\). Apply Theorem 3.1 with \([a,b] = [\lambda_k(A), \lambda_1(A)]\). By Weyl, every discarded eigenvalue of \(\hat A\) satisfies \(\lambda_j(\hat A) \le \lambda_{k+1}(A) + \|E\| = a - (\delta_0 - \|E\|)\). So \(\delta = \delta_0 - \|E\| \ge \delta_0/2\) works.

If instead \(\|E\| > \delta_0/2\), the operator-norm bound exceeds \(1 \ge \|\sin\Theta\|\), so it holds trivially.

The Frobenius bound follows from \(\|\sin\Theta\|_F \le \sqrt k\,\|\sin\Theta\|\). \(\blacksquare\)

Yu, Wang & Samworth (2015) prove the sharper \(\|\sin\Theta\|_F \le 2\min(\sqrt k\,\|E\|, \|E\|_F)/\delta_0\) and handle eigenvalues in the middle of the spectrum, not just the top \(k\). Combined with Lemma 2.1(3), for some unitary \(O\), \[ \|\hat VO - V\|_F \le \frac{2\sqrt{2k}\,\|E\|}{\delta_0}. \]

4. Application sketch: why spectral clustering works

This is the template shared by Ng–Jordan–Weiss, the analysis in von Luxburg’s tutorial, and the stochastic block model analyses of Rohe–Chatterjee–Yu and Lei–Rinaldo.

  1. Ideal matrix. Take \(k\) clusters with no edges between them, or a population matrix \(A = \mathbb{E}[\hat A]\) with block structure. Then the top-\(k\) eigenvectors of the appropriate matrix have rows that take only \(k\) distinct values, one per cluster.
    • Unnormalized block model: node \(i\) in cluster \(j\) has row \(\propto e_j^\top/\sqrt{n_j}\), up to a rotation.
    • Normalized Laplacian (Ng–Jordan–Weiss): rows are rescaled by the square roots of the degrees. After row-normalization, they become \(k\) mutually orthogonal unit vectors.
    Let \(\Delta\) be the minimum distance between distinct rows.
  2. Eigengap. Compute or lower-bound \(\delta_0 = \lambda_k(A) - \lambda_{k+1}(A)\). For a disconnected ideal graph, this is controlled by the spectral gaps of the individual clusters.
  3. Perturbation size. Bound \(\|E\| = \|\hat A - A\|\). In the deterministic setting, \(E\) is the between-cluster weight. In the random setting, a matrix concentration inequality gives \(\|E\| = O(\sqrt{\text{max expected degree}})\) with high probability.
  4. Davis–Kahan. \(\|\hat VO - V\|_F \le 2\sqrt{2k}\,\|E\|/\delta_0\).
  5. Rounding. Call node \(i\) bad if its row satisfies \(\|(\hat VO)_i - V_i\| \ge \Delta/2\). By Markov’s inequality on the squared row errors, \[ \#\text{bad nodes} \le \frac{4\,\|\hat VO - V\|_F^2}{\Delta^2} \le \frac{32\,k\,\|E\|^2}{\Delta^2\,\delta_0^2}. \] Assigning each good node to the nearest true center labels it correctly. For the centers found by (approximate) \(k\)-means, an extra argument is needed, which loses constant factors (Lei & Rinaldo 2015, Lemma 5.3).

The row-wise conclusion in step 5 comes from a Frobenius bound via Markov. Bounding every row individually needs \(\ell_{2\to\infty}\) or entrywise perturbation theory (Cape–Tang–Priebe; Abbe–Fan–Wang–Zhong), which is a natural “further directions” pointer.

5. Sources and how they fit together

Notes for planning the lecture. Titles, venues, and years are from memory. The Ng–Jordan–Weiss citation of Stewart & Sun in particular should be checked before it goes on a syllabus.

The original theorem.

  • C. Davis & W. M. Kahan, “The rotation of eigenvectors by a perturbation. III,” SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 7 (1970). The source of the \(\sin\Theta\), \(\tan\Theta\), \(\sin 2\Theta\) family of theorems, stated for operators on Hilbert space. Heavy going; better cited than assigned.
  • G. W. Stewart & J.-G. Sun, Matrix Perturbation Theory (Academic Press, 1990). The standard matrix-analysis reference for invariant-subspace perturbation. This is what Ng–Jordan–Weiss cite.
  • R. Bhatia, Matrix Analysis (GTM 169), Ch. VII. Clean proofs of the \(\sin\Theta\) theorems via Sylvester equations, including the operator-norm subtleties. The proof above follows this route.

Ng, Jordan & Weiss — the classical application. “On Spectral Clustering: Analysis and an Algorithm” (NIPS 2001).

  • Introduces the now-standard algorithm: normalized affinity \(D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\), top-\(k\) eigenvectors, row-normalization, \(k\)-means.
  • The analysis treats the observed affinity matrix as a perturbation of the ideal block-diagonal one. It applies a Stewart–Sun subspace bound (a Davis–Kahan \(\sin\Theta\) theorem, not cited under that name) with an eigengap supplied by assumptions (A1)–(A4) on within-cluster connectivity and between-cluster weight.
  • Conclusion: the row-normalized embedding clusters tightly around \(k\) mutually orthogonal points.
  • Row-normalization is exactly what makes the ideal rows orthogonal unit vectors despite degree heterogeneity.
  • Good for the lecture as the historical origin. The assumptions are somewhat opaque, so a cleaner restatement is worth doing in class.

von Luxburg, “A Tutorial on Spectral Clustering,” Statistics and Computing (2007).

  • The perturbation-theory section states Davis–Kahan explicitly and explains why the eigengap between \(\lambda_k\) and \(\lambda_{k+1}\) is the right quantity.
  • Also discusses when the argument is shaky: the ideal eigenvectors must be piecewise constant, and the perturbation must be small in operator norm.
  • Probably the easiest version to present, and a good reading assignment.

Stochastic block model analyses — the modern template.

  • Rohe, Chatterjee & Yu, “Spectral clustering and the high-dimensional stochastic blockmodel,” Annals of Statistics (2011). Normalized Laplacian under the SBM, with the number of clusters allowed to grow. Uses concentration + Davis–Kahan + counting misclustered nodes.
  • Lei & Rinaldo, “Consistency of spectral clustering in stochastic block models,” Annals of Statistics (2015). Sharper, cleaner version for the adjacency matrix. Includes the approximate-\(k\)-means rounding lemma used in step 5 above. Best source for a complete, rigorous end-to-end proof.

Yu, Wang & Samworth, “A useful variant of the Davis–Kahan theorem for statisticians,” Biometrika (2015).

  • Requires an eigengap only for the population matrix \(A\), not for the noisy \(\hat A\). This is exactly what applications need, since the observed spectrum is not under our control.
  • Also gives the \(\min(\sqrt k\|E\|, \|E\|_F)\) form, and a version for singular vectors (Wedin-type).
  • Short, and a good reading.

Peng, Sun & Zanetti, “Partitioning well-clustered graphs: spectral clustering works!” (COLT 2015; journal version in SIAM J. Comput.).

  • A contrasting, worst-case (non-probabilistic) approach. Assumes a gap between \(\lambda_{k+1}\) of the normalized Laplacian and the \(k\)-way expansion constant \(\rho(k)\).
  • Proves a structure theorem: the bottom-\(k\) eigenvectors are close to cluster indicator vectors, and vice versa. This is argued directly, not through a black-box Davis–Kahan bound.
  • Connects naturally to the higher-order Cheeger inequality (Lee–Oveis Gharan–Trevisan) and fits a course with a spectral graph theory emphasis.
  • Useful to contrast with the Davis–Kahan route: which quantity plays the role of the eigengap, and why.

Further directions (row-wise / entrywise control).

  • Cape, Tang & Priebe, “The two-to-infinity norm and singular subspace geometry…,” Annals of Statistics (2019).
  • Abbe, Fan, Wang & Zhong, “Entrywise eigenvector analysis of random matrices with low expected rank,” Annals of Statistics (2020).
  • These give per-row bounds, and hence exact recovery results, where Davis–Kahan plus Markov only bounds the number of misclustered nodes.

6. Exercises

  1. Complete the proof of Lemma 2.1(2): compute \(VV^* - \hat V\hat V^*\) in the basis \([\hat V\ \hat V_\perp]\) and find its eigenvalues.
  2. Show that the gap condition in Theorem 3.1 cannot be dropped: for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), construct \(A, E\) with \(\|E\| = \varepsilon\) and \(\|\sin\Theta\| = 1\) for the top eigenvectors.
  3. Show that the constant in Theorem 3.1 is sharp: find \(2\times2\) examples with \(\|\sin\Theta\|\) arbitrarily close to \(\|E\|/\delta\).
  4. Let \(G\) be the disjoint union of two copies of a \(d\)-regular expander on \(n/2\) vertices, whose second adjacency eigenvalue is \(\lambda < d\). Add an arbitrary set of edges between the copies with maximum degree \(d'\). Use Corollary 3.3 (with \(k = 2\)) and the rounding argument of Section 4 to bound the number of misclassified vertices in terms of \(n\), \(d'\), and \(d - \lambda\). (Hint: \(\|E\| \le d'\).)
  5. (SVD version.) State and prove an analogue of Theorem 3.1 for left and right singular subspaces of a rectangular matrix, by applying it to the self-adjoint dilation \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & M \\ M^* & 0\end{pmatrix}\).
