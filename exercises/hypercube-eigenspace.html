Exercise: Which Eigenvector? The Hypercube

Recommended exercise for Lecture 6

The Fiedler eigenspace of the cube is d-dimensional, and most vectors in it give bad sweep cuts.

On the cycle, both sides of Cheeger’s inequality are visible, but the Fiedler vector is easy to use. On the hypercube, the lower side of Cheeger is tight, \(\Phi=\lambda/2\), yet the eigenvector you choose matters: the second eigenvalue has multiplicity \(d\), and the quality of the sweep cut depends on which vector of the eigenspace the algorithm returns.

Setup. \(Q_d\) has vertex set \(\{0,1\}^d\), with \(z\sim z'\) iff they differ in exactly one coordinate. It is \(d\)-regular with \(n=2^d\) vertices. For \(S\subseteq[d]\) let \(\chi_S(z)=(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}z_i}\). Write \(\chi_i=\chi_{\{i\}}\). All graph quantities are for unit weights, and \(\lambda=\lambda_2(L_{rw})\).

(a) The spectrum. Show that \(\chi_S\) is an eigenvector of \(L_{rw}(Q_d)\) with eigenvalue \(2|S|/d\), and that these \(2^d\) functions are a basis. Conclude \(\lambda=2/d\), with a \(d\)-dimensional eigenspace spanned by \(\chi_1,\dots,\chi_d\).

(b) The good eigenvector. Show that the sweep sets of \(\chi_i\) consist of a single set \(\{z_i=0\}\) (up to complement), and that it has \(\Phi=1/d\). Use the easy side of Cheeger to conclude \(\Phi(Q_d)=1/d\).

(c) The bad eigenvector. Let \(f=\sum_{i=1}^d\chi_i\), which is also a Fiedler vector. Show that \(f(z)=d-2|z|\), where \(|z|\) is the Hamming weight, so the threshold sets of \(f\) are the Hamming balls \(B_r=\{z:|z|\le r\}\) (and their complements). Show that for \(r<d/2\), \[ \Phi(B_r)=\frac{\binom dr\,(d-r)}{d\sum_{j\le r}\binom dj}. \]

(d) Every sweep cut of \(f\) is bad. Show that there is a constant \(c>0\) such that \(\Phi(B_r)\ge c/\sqrt d\) for every \(0\le r<d/2\). (Hence every sweep cut of \(f\) has conductance \(\Omega(\sqrt{\Phi(Q_d)})\), although \(\lambda=2/d\) and \(\Phi(Q_d)=1/d\).) Which constant does the exact asymptotics give? Compare with Cheeger’s guarantee \(\Phi(S)\le\sqrt{2\lambda}=2/\sqrt d\).

(e) Numerics. For \(d=8,10,12,14\), compute the best sweep cut conductance of

  1. \(f=\chi_1\) (use threshold sets only);
  2. \(f=\sum_i\chi_i\) (threshold sets only, i.e. Hamming balls);
  3. \(f=\sum_ig_i\chi_i\) for \(10\) random Gaussian vectors \(g\).

Plot the three against \(d\), together with \(1/d\) and \(1/\sqrt d\). What do you observe for the Gaussian vectors?

(f) Discussion. Compare with the tree \(\square\) path example (Exercise). In which sense is the loss here caused by degeneracy, and there by near-degeneracy? Can an algorithm that only sees \(\lambda_2\) and one eigenvector, computed by the power method from a random start, avoid this loss on the cube? What does the power method converge to?

Hint for (a). \(L_{rw}=I-\frac1dA\) and \(A\chi_S(z)=\sum_i\chi_S(z\oplus e_i)=\chi_S(z)\sum_i(\pm1)\), where the sign is \(-1\) iff \(i\in S\). For the basis, the \(\chi_S\) are orthogonal characters of \(\mathbb Z_2^d\).

Hint for (b). For the lower bound on \(\Phi(Q_d)\), use \(\Phi\ge\lambda/2\).

Hint for (c). \(\chi_i(z)=1-2z_i\). The set \(B_r\) has all vertices of weight \(\le r\), so its boundary consists of the edges between weight \(r\) and weight \(r+1\): \(\binom dr(d-r)\) of them. Also \(\mathrm{vol}(B_r)=d|B_r|\), and for \(r<d/2\) this is at most half of \(\mathrm{vol}(V)\).

Hint for (d). Two regimes. If \(d-2r\ge\sqrt d\): for \(j\le r\), \(\binom d{j-1}/\binom dj=j/(d-j+1)\le q:=r/(d-r+1)<1\), so \(\sum_{j\le r}\binom dj\le\binom dr/(1-q)\), which gives \(\Phi(B_r)\ge\frac{(d-r)(d-2r+1)}{d(d-r+1)}\ge\frac{d-2r+1}{2d}\ge\frac1{2\sqrt d}\). If \(d-2r<\sqrt d\): use \(\binom dr\ge c_0 2^d/\sqrt d\) in this range (Stirling) and \(\sum_{j\le r}\binom dj\le2^{d-1}\). The exact asymptotic uses \(\binom d{d/2}\sim 2^d\sqrt{2/(\pi d)}\).

Hint for (e). For the Gaussian vectors, sort the \(2^d\) values \(f(z)\) and compute the cut of each prefix by counting, for every edge \(\{z,z\oplus e_b\}\), the prefix sizes it crosses (a difference array of length \(2^d\) does this in \(O(d2^d)\)).

Hint for (f). The power method started from a random vector converges to a random direction in the eigenspace (the projection of the start onto it), so for large \(d\) it behaves like a Gaussian vector \(g\) in (e).
